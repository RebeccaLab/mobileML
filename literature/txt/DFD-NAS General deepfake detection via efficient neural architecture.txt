A B S T R A C T
As the saying goes, ‘‘seeing is believing’’. However, with the development of digital face editing tools, we can
no longer trust what we can see. Though face forgery detection has made promising progress, most current
methods are manually designed by human experts, which is labor-intensive. In this paper, we develop an end-
to-end framework based on neural architecture search (NAS) for deepfake detection, which can automatically
design network architectures without human intervention. First, a forgery-oriented search space is created to
choose appropriate operations for this task, which facilitates the search process in finding the most valuable
gradient information for face forgery detection. Second, inspired by the fact that the gap between training
error and test error is a good indicator of generalization ability for a classification task, we propose a novel
performance estimation metric. This metric encourages the error in the performance estimation phase to be
close to the error in the search phase, which guides the search process to select more general models. The
cross-dataset search is also considered to develop more general architectures. Eventually, we design a cell
cascaded pyramid network (C2PN) for final detection, which aggregates multiscale features for performance
improvements. Compared with state-of-the-art networks artificially designed, our method achieves competitive
performance in both in-dataset and cross-dataset scenarios.
1. Introduction
The emergence of face forgery technology has caused serious con-
cerns in the whole society [1–5]. First, using photos of political figures,
stars, and other people may incur legal issues, such as reputations,
portrait rights, intellectual properties, and so on. Second, videos of im-
portant persons are highly valuable intelligence. The abuse of counter-
feit technology can lead to the spread of false information, even cause
diplomatic crises. In addition, privacy disclosure may further affect
asset security and personal safety. The fraud of such vivid deepfakes
will cause a number of legal, social, and ethical problems.
To fight against the abuse of facial manipulation technologies, many
pioneers have made significant progress [6–8]. However, the problem
is far from completely solved. On one hand, though decent performance
has been achieved when training and tests are implemented in the same
dataset, the generalization ability in the cross-dataset scenario is still
challenging. There is an urgent demand for more general architectures.
On the other hand, most of these methods are hand-crafted by human

summary, our contributions are as follows:
• We propose a novel end-to-end NAS framework to automatically
design a network architecture for face forgery detection. To bet-

gradually select appropriate gradient information. To the best of
our knowledge, it is the first NAS-based method with specialized

2. Related works
2.1. Face forgery detection
Some prior arts are proposed for face forgery detection [2–5,14]. In
this subsection, we summarize the previous works by the architectures
they choose. In the early years, researchers explore hand-crafted fea-
tures for face forgery detection. Qi et al. [15] propose DeepRhythm
to classify deepfakes by monitoring the heartbeat rhythms. Agarwal
et al. [16] detect face forgery by modeling facial expressions and move-
ments that character the speaking patterns. Yang et al. [17] observe
that deepfakes are generated by splicing synthesized face regions, and
present a forensics pipeline by 3D head pose estimation.
The performance of the above methods based on hand-crafted fea-
tures is quickly surpassed by the forensic framework designed by deep
learning. Nirkin et al. [18] design a method for detecting fake videos
using a single frame. This method includes a face recognition network
and a context recognition network for facial features. Yang et al. [19]
adopt central difference convolution and atrous spatial pyramid pooling
(ASPP) to propose a multi-scale texture difference model for face
forgery detection. Yu et al. [13] introduce a specific forgery feature
extractor and a common forgery feature extractor for learning uni-
versal forensic features. Wang et al. [20] use a cross-modality knowl-
edge distillation method for near-infrared face forgery authentication.
Nevertheless, as the structure of neural networks becomes more and
more complex, the hyperparameters that need to be adjusted increase
geometrically. Designing networks manually for deepfake detection
becomes labor-consuming due to repetitive experiments.
With the popularity of Transformer architecture, more and more de-
tection methods based on this technique appeared. Miao et al. [21] at-
tempt to exploit this problem from both spatial and frequency domains.
They design a high-frequency fine-grained Transformer network, which
consists of central difference attention and high-frequency wavelet
samplers. Zhao et al. [22] employ an interpretable spatial–temporal
video Transformer to capture spatial artifacts and temporal incon-
sistency for robust deepfake detection. This framework is composed
of a decomposed spatial–temporal self-attention and a self-subtract
mechanism. Though Transformer-based methods bring performance
improvements, the architectures designed by human experts require
substantial experience and repeated experiments.
Adopting NAS framework for face forgery detection receives rel-
atively less research attention. Liu et al. [23] develop an automated
deepfake detection framework using common convolutional layers as
the search space. However, ordinary convolution is not specially de-
signed to extract forgery traces. There is still room for performance
improvement if the search space is appropriately selected.
2.2. Neural architecture search
Neural architecture search has proposed for automatically design
neural networks [24]. The NASNet [25] is the first algorithm to use
modular search, which greatly reduces the computation time due to
normal cells and reduction cells. The ENAS [26] samples subgraphs
from a large hypergraph, and the operations in the subgraph inherit
the weights of the hypergraph. This approach facilitates the sharing of
weights between different structures and greatly reduces the training
time of neural network structures in performance prediction. The Prox-
ylessNAS [27] searches based on MobileNetV2, changing the number
of convolutional layers, convolutional kernel sizes, and the number of
filters in its internal MBConv unit. All MBConv units have no shared

3. The proposed method
In this section, we first revisit the differentiable architecture search
process. Then, we describe the search space, cell topology, perfor-
mance estimation strategy, search strategy, and cross-dataset search,
respectively. After that, we suggest the C2PN for final detection. Even-
tually, we also discuss the novelty of the proposed method. The whole
framework is illustrated in Fig. 2.
3.1. Revisit differentiable architecture search
The process of neural architecture search mainly includes three
parts: search space, search strategy and performance estimation. First,
the search space defines all the candidate neural network operations or
components. Then, the corresponding search strategy selects a certain
neural network structure from the space. This intermediate network is
evaluated by several performance indicators. Finally, the evaluation re-
sults are fed back to the search strategy until the optimal architectures
are obtained.
Differentiable architecture search (DARTS) regards operation selec-
tion as a weighted combination of operator sets, so that the search space
is relaxed to be continuous [29]. In this way, the hyperparameters can
be calculated differentiably by gradient descent. The basic component
in the architecture is a cell, which can be considered as a directed
acyclic graph (DAG) with 𝑁nodes. A topological node represents for a
layer in the neural network. Usually, each cell is composed of two input
nodes, one output node, and the remaining intermediate nodes. For the
𝑗th intermediate node, its output is a summation of all the forward
results of preceding nodes,

3.7. Cell cascaded pyramid network
After obtaining the cells, we connect them in series to detect face
forgery videos. Since there are a large number of connections between
nodes at the beginning of the search phase, it is computationally
consuming. We use a relatively lightweight network, which contains
two groups of cells, for architecture search. Each group is composed of
two normal cells and a reduction cell. In total, six cells are used for the
search stage, which is illustrated at the top of Fig. 2.
Multiscale information is vital in deepfake forensics [19]. We try
to preserve this valuable multiscale information for the final detection.
Thus, we use a deeper network to gradually decode the feature maps.
As shown in the bottom of Fig. 2, the detection network contains
four groups of cells. Each group has two normal cells followed by
a reduction cell. We adopt the cells obtained from the above search
stage without any training weights. The detection network is trained
on benchmark datasets for in-dataset and cross-dataset tests.
3.8. Discussion of the novelty
In this paper, we collect the forgery-oriented search space. The
manipulation traces are too fragile to be discovered by ordinary layers
in the neural networks. CDC can be used for face anti-spoofing, which
indicates its effectiveness in extracting face features. However, previous
methods only consider the fixed value of 𝜃of the CDC. The value
of 𝜃controls the proportion of gradient information. The gradient
information plays an important role in deepfake detection. Thus, we
gradually change the value of 𝜃in CDC in the search space. This

4. Experiment
In this section, we describe the experiments in detail. First, we
establish the experimental setup, including datasets, evaluation metrics,
compared methods, and implementation details. Then, we analyze the
comparison with state-of-the-art approaches. After that, we give the
ablation study by trying different choices of search space, search strat-
egy, cell connection, and input size. Finally, we illustrate visualization
analysis and discuss the limitations.
4.1. Experimental setup
4.1.1. Datasets
We evaluate our method on four publicly available datasets. Face-
Forensics++ (FF++) [41] consists of four forgery approaches, e.g.,
DeepFakes (DF), Face2Face (F2F), FaceSwap (FS), and NeuralTextures
(NT). This dataset covers two compression rates, e.g., high-quality
(C23) and low-quality (C40). Celeb-DF [42] contains 590 real and 5639
forged videos totally from YouTube website. These clips are generated
by advanced synthesis techniques. WildDeepfake [43] collects 7314
real-world face sequences from the Internet. The duration of the video
clip changes dramatically. DFDC-preview [44] is composed of more
than 5000 videos with various manipulation methods. The faces in the
video may be partially forged.
In-dataset search only uses FF++ for searching neural architectures.
Cross-dataset search adopts four datasets alternately. For in-dataset
scenes, after determining the network structure, the models are trained
and tested on the above four datasets, respectively. For cross-dataset
experiments, we train the networks on FF++ dataset and test on
the other three datasets to verify the generalization ability. In FF++
dataset, following the official splits, we use 720 videos for training,
140 videos for validation, and 140 videos for test. Each video takes 20
frames for training and 10 frames for validation and test. For the other
three datasets, we divided them into the training set, validation set, and
test set according to the ratio of 8:1:1.

4.1.3. Compared methods
The proposed method is compared with 23 face forgery detection
frameworks, including
• Meso4 [45] focuses on the mesoscopic properties of images.
• MesoInception4 [45] replaces the first two convolutional layers
of Meso4 by a variant of the inception module [46].
• RNN [47] uses a CNN to extract frame-level features, and train a
RNN that learns to classify a video.
• FWA [48] is inspired by the limited resolutions of the generated
images of deepfake algorithm.
• Xception [41] is a face forensic method based on Xception-
Net [49].
• Multi-Task [50] denotes the multi-task learning network includ-
ing an encoder and a Y-shaped decoder.
• Capsule [51] equips with a capsule network to detect various
kinds of spoofs.
• EfficientNet-B4 [52] is a forensic pipeline, adopting Efficient-
Net [52] as backbones.
• F3-Net [53] is short for frequency in face forgery network, which
employing two frequency-aware clues.
• Multi-Attention [54] framework consists of spatial attention
heads, textural feature enhancement block, and multi-level fea-
ture aggregation.
• LTW [55] is based on the meta-weight learning algorithm, which
configures different weights for face images.
• FT-TS [56] is composed of two-stream backbones, analyzing from
frame-level and temporality-level of videos.
• AutoDFD [23] employs neural architecture search for deepfake
detection.
• FInfer [57] is a frame inference-based detection method for
high-visual-quality deepfake detection.
• RECCE [58] focuses on the common compact representations of
genuine faces based on reconstruction-classification learning.
• NoiseDF [59] emphasizes the underlying forensic noise traces left
behind the deepfake videos.
• MRL [60] formulates deepfake detection as a graph classification
problem.
• F2Trans

4.3. Ablation study
In this subsection, we analyze the necessity of some key settings in
our method, including search space, search strategy, cell connection,
and input size. In the following ablation experiments, we only consider
the performance of in-dataset evaluation. The final detection networks
are trained on Celeb-DF [42].
4.3.1. Different search space
The forgery-oriented search space plays an important role in our
method. As shown in Table 4, our search space is more effective
than vanilla CNN in PC-DARTS [39]. Central difference has a positive
influence apparently. Various 𝜃values also perform better than the
default value 𝜃= 0.7.
4.3.2. Different search strategy
We also compare the effects of the search strategy in Table 5. ‘‘IND’’
denotes the proposed performance estimation indicator. The proposed
method adopts pruning and a novel performance estimation indicator,
making the whole algorithm more effective for this forensics task.
Concretely, pruning saves nearly half of the search time. The proposed
new indicator also contributes to better performance.
4.3.3. Different cell connection
Since the multiscale information conveys vital clues for forgeries
[19], we gradually downsample the resolutions between cells. In Table 6,
‘‘N’’ represents a normal cell, and ‘‘R’’ denotes a reduction cell. The
number of reduction cells has an impact on performance. As the number
of reduction cells increases, the results become better. The proposed
C2PN adopts four reduction cells (N×2+R+N×2+R+N×2+R+N×2 +R)
to progressively downsample feature maps. This choice obtains the best
results among all the approaches, including the cell connection method
in PC-DARTS [39].
4.3.4. Different input size
In this field, the input size of most methods is 256 × 256. If the
input size is 256 × 256 for training, the batch size can only be set to
4, which takes about 2.6 h to train an epoch. If using a 64 × 64 image
for training, the batch size can be set to 48, which only takes about
8 min to train an epoch. When the input is 64 × 64, the FLOPs of our
model is 1.744G and the parameter quantity is 13.37M. Due to GPU
memory limitations, we choose smaller images as inputs. Though our
input image resolution is smaller than other baselines, we still achieved
competitive performance.

4.5. Discussion of the limitation
The main objective of this article is to explore the potential of NAS
for deepfake detection without manual intervention. Nonetheless, the
automatically searched neural network is still based on the CNN ar-
chitecture. Compared with graph-based frameworks and Transformer-
based models, our method did not show significant performance im-
provement on some datasets.
However, our method utilizes fewer parameter quantities and
achieves comparable results. The parameter quantity of our model is
13.37M.
SFDG [65] adopts EfficientNet-b4 (#Params: >19M) as backbone.
Our method performs better in cross-dataset test on WildDeepfake
dataset (AUC: 74.71 vs. 69.27) and DFDC dataset (AUC: 75.79 vs.
73.64). PEL [66] employs a dual-stream framework using EffcientNet-
b4 as backbones (#Params: >38M). We obtain better performance
in cross-dataset circumstances on Celeb-DF (AUC: 72.48 vs. 69.18),
WildDeepfake (AUC: 74.71 vs. 67.39), DFDC (AUC: 75.79 vs. 63.31).
Compared with UIA-ViT [67], we obtain comparable results in DFDC-
pre dataset (AUC: 75.79 vs. 75.80) in cross-dataset setting. The param-
eter quantity of our network is much fewer than the ViT-Base structure
in UIA-ViT (#Params: 13.37M vs. 86M).
In practical application scenarios on mobile devices, methods with
smaller parameter quantities are more convenient to deploy and have
better practicality.
5. Conclusion
In this paper, we propose an end-to-end face forgery detection
framework based on NAS, which can automatically design suitable
neural networks for this task. First, we create a forgery-oriented search
space to find appropriate operations for deepfake detection. Then, we
take the actual estimation error as guidance to improve the search gen-
eralizability. Besides ordinary search manners, we also consider cross-
dataset search to obtain a more general neural network architecture.
Eventually, we connect the cells obtained by NAS with skip connections
in a cascaded pyramid way. Extensive experimental results demonstrate
the proposed method achieves competitive performance compared with
state-of-the-art approaches, especially good generalization ability in
cross-dataset settings.
In the future, since the Transformer architecture [68] has shown
significant performance improvements in many fields, we will try to
further expand the search space to make DFD-NAS compatible with
Transformer modules and thus search for more promising architectures.
Furthermore, considering the convergence can indicate the superior-
ity of the searched architectures in an unsupervised NAS setting, we
would like to extend our NAS framework in the unsupervised learning
manner [69].
