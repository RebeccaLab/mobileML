Abstract— The rapid growth of deepfake technology has 
raised critical concerns regarding misinformation, identity 
theft, and cybersecurity risks. Detecting these manipulated 
media requires sophisticated algorithms capable of effectively 
distinguishing between real and fake content. This study 
provides a comparative analysis  of seven convolutional neural 
network (CNN) architectures—ResNet50, VGG16, VGG19, 
Xception, InceptionV3, MobileNetV2, and MobileNetV1—using 
the challenging Celeb-DF v2 dataset for deepfake detection. 
While CNNs have been widely studied for deepfake detection, 
this work focuses on the trade-offs between model accuracy and 
computational efficiency, offering insights into how lightweight 
models can be optimized for real-world applications. 
Performance was assessed using key metrics such as accuracy, 
precision, recall, loss, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC. Among the 
models tested, MobileNetV1 achieved the best performance, 
with an accuracy of 98.24% and perfect scores of 1.000 for both 
ROC-AUC and PR-AUC. MobileNetV1 also demonstrated a 
significantly lower loss of 0.0535, outperforming other models in 
error minimization during training. These findings provide 
valuable guidance for selecting efficient yet high-performing 
CNN 
models 
in 
deepfake 
detection, 
particularly 
in 
environments where computational resources are limited.

I. INTRODUCTION 
Deepfake technology is based on artificial intelligence 
(AI) and deep learning algorithms such as Generative 
Adversarial Networks (GANs), and is one of the most 
discussed technologies in recent times. It allows constructing 
hyper-realistic artificial images, videos and audio, which are 
challenging to differentiate with real ones. GANs are neural 
networks containing two neural networks: one producing the 
content and the other differentiating between the content and 
the real media have achieved great progress in the production 
of highly realistic synthetic media. It has become more open 
source technology and now has given new opportunities in 
the entertainment and creative sectors, i.e., virtual actors and 
interactive media. Nevertheless, with the development of the 
deepfake technology, the possibility of its abuse has become 
a critical issue of concern when it comes to privacy, security, 
and the quality of digital media. Due to the popularity of 
deepfake generation software, there are various abuses of the 
technology. Deepfakes have been used in political 
disinformation campaigns as the most pressing problem, 
where manipulated media is used to present false information 
about people or false news, which may affect elections and 
opinion of the population [1]. What makes these videos go 
viral is that they create confusion and cast their doubts on the 
originality of digital content. Cyberbullying is another area of 
involvement of deepfake technology, whereby defamatory 
information is transmitted with the help of manipulated 
videos and audio. This may lead to serious emotional damage 
and reputation loss [2]. Also, scams have also exploited 
deepfake technology, such as by impersonating executives in 
a video or voice call to lure employees into sharing sensitive 
information, which has led to financial loss and security 
breaches [3].

Deepfakes are not only a threat to cybersecurity but also 
a major issue with regard to personal harassment and 
misinformation. With progress in the technology of deepfake 
generating systems, it is becoming harder to use digital media 
with trust, particularly when it comes to such a situation as a 
video meeting or a digital transaction [4]. Deepfake 
technology allows a person to imitate the voice or the face of 
a person in a convincing way and can be used by 
cybercriminals to make individuals share sensitive data, 
which can be seen as problematic in terms of the safety of 
online communication and money transfer via the Internet . 
Conventional methods of detection, including examination of 
image metadata or the presence of compression artifacts, 
have also been shown to be ineffective against quality 
deepfakes [5]. The most recent state-of-the-art in deep 
learning, specifically using Convolutional Neural Networks 
(CNNs) has rendered the possible detection of subtle 
inconsistencies in deepfake media that cannot be detected by 
the naked eye. CNNs are capable of automatically extracting 
high-level representations on data and recognizing complex 
patterns, e.g. irregular facial movements and irregular 
lighting [6],[7].Transfer learning has countered this, 
however, by demonstrating itself to be an important way of 
deepfake detection, where deepfake narrow datasets can be 
fine-tuned on large datasets such as ImageNet. It enhances 
the detection rate and saves the training time, particularly 
when the labeled data is limited, and enables models to be 
adapted to small visual artifacts associated with new methods 
of deepfakes generation [5], [8]. 
Although the progress of deepfake detection with CNNs 
has been achieved, there are still gaps in literature. Most of 
the research work devotes effort to attain model accuracy, 
thus there exists no such review to assess the trade-offs 
between the computation efficiency and performance that is 
important in the real-world application. Moreover, despite the 
success of CNNs, such as ResNet and VGG16, not many 
studies have critically evaluated the differences in the relative 
performance of different CNN architectures on different data 
sets. Also, the models usually train on the particular 
properties of the data, which restricts their generalizing 
capabilities and makes them unable to succeed on new 
unexpected deepfakes. [9]. This paper aims to address these 
gaps through the following contributions: 
• 
Comparative Evaluation of CNN Architectures 
• 
Trade-offs Between Accuracy and Efficiency 
• 
Address Dataset Challenges and Overfitting Using 
Transfer Learning 
II. METHODOLOGY   
The proposed system of deepfake detection is structured 
as a pipeline of steps systematically preparing and pre-
processing data before deep learning-based classification. It 
starts by decomposing video, in which samples of the Celeb-
DF V2 are broken down into separate frames. To achieve a 
sound evaluation, redundant frames are removed and dataset 
is balanced to have a proportional distribution of authentic 
and manipulated samples in training, validation and testing 
subsets. Every single frame that is extracted is preprocessed 
and this procedure includes face detection, cropping, resizing 
and normalization so that models concentrate on facial 
artifacts associated with manipulation instead of background 
noise. This is done to decrease computational work and to 
increase the quality of input data. The fined frames are 
subsequently fed into a collection of state-of-the-art 
convolutional neural network (CNN) models, such as 
ResNet50, VGG16, VGG19, Xception, InceptionV3, 
MobileNetV1, and MobileNetV2. We pre-train them using 
ImageNet weights, and they are further trained to perform 
binary classification between real and fake images, using 
transfer learning as a way to speed up convergence and 
generalization. The tuning of hyperparameters (e.g., batch 
size, learning rate, and number of epochs) during supervised 
training is used to obtain optimal performance, whereas 
regularization methods are used to reduce overfitting. It is 
evaluated based on several performance measures, such as 
accuracy precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the ROC 
curve (AUC).

A. Dataset 
This paper uses the Celeb-DF v2 dataset which is one of 
the most difficult and realistic datasets in deepfake detection 
literature. Celeb-DF v2 was built to correct the weaknesses of 
previous datasets, which tended to have low visual quality 
and could have obvious artifacts like color dismatch, splicing 
edges, and unnatural face poses. It is composed of 590 real 
videos, 300 YouTube real clips, and 5639 high-quality 
deepfake videos that have been created with an improved 
synthesis process that minimizes the typical flaws of previous 
datasets [10],[11]. 
B. Pre-processing  
Once the datasets were collected, some pre-processing 
operations were implemented to make sure the frames 
extracted were consistent, well-balanced, and could be 
trained on deep learning models. Such measures reduced the 
noise due to unrelated parts, handled imbalance in the classes,

1) Frame Extraction 
In the initial step, individual frames that comprised every 
video in the Celeb-DF v2 dataset were systematically broken 
down to allow a frame level analysis. Deepfake artifacts can 
be usually subtle and they do not necessarily appear across 
the whole video; instead, they can be found in short segments, 
or even just a frame. Frame extraction also made sure that all 
potential cases of manipulated content could be processed 
separately, which made the dataset more robust. Upon 
extraction, frames were separated into three subsets such as 
training, validation, and testing that play different roles in the 
process of model building and testing. Table I shows that 
there were 8,048 fake and 8,072 real frames in the training 
set, 1,772 fake and 1,761 real frames in the validation set and 
1,708 fake and 1,695 real frames in the testing set. A total of 
23,056 frames were harvested, split into 70 percent training, 
15 percent validation and 15 percent testing, which gave both 
balance and representativeness.

2) Frame Balancing 
A frame balancing strategy was used to eliminate bias 
towards the majority class. Most deepfake datasets are not 
balanced between real and fake data, and this can bias 
paradigms to falsely predict a particular classification. With 
this end in view, the number of real and fake frames was 
almost equal and kept in each subset. Table I shows that the 
distribution was almost perfectly balanced with almost no 
differences between real and fake samples in terms of 
training, validation, and testing sets. This balancing measure 
made sure that the models learned discriminative features, 
and this results in higher reliability of evaluation measures 
like accuracy, precision and recall. 
3) Face Region Extraction 
As the background factors can be excluded as the 
manipulations are made mostly to the face like clothing or 
background factors will be excluded. An efficient face 
detecting algorithm was used to identify and extract the face 
of each frame. This step minimized computation and 
removed irrelevant noise which compelled the models to pay 
attention to manipulation-specific information including 
texture anomalies, blending artifacts and inconsistencies in 
lighting. The presence of these cleaner inputs and higher 
detection rates was achieved by isolating the face and making 
the dataset cleaner. 
4) Face Normalization and Resizing 
Lastly, cropped faces were standardized and resized to 
make all CNN architectures receive standardized inputs. 
Light variations, camera quality and contrast differences were 
smoothed by equalizing pixel intensity values which is 
normally within the interval [0,1]. Faces were then cropped 
and down sampled to 224x224 pixels, matching the 
dimension of other architectures like ResNet, VGG and 
MobileNet. 
This 
standardization 
increased 
the 
reproducibility of models, speeded up convergence and 
guaranteed fair comparison between models. 
C. Model Architecture 
The proposed deepfake detection architecture will be able 
to process and classify video as real or fake with advanced 
deep learning models. The system is separated into the 
Preprocessing, Model Training and Evaluation and Testing 
phases. Training flow the model is trained on the 
preprocessed dataset that has been frame extracted, face 
cropped, normalized and resized. Some of the deep learning 
architectures that we used are ResNet50, VGG16, VGG19, 
Xception, InceptionV3, MobileNetV1, and MobileNetV2, 
which we used to extract features. MobileNetV1 turned out 
to be the most successful after training with a high accuracy, 
precision, recall, and AUC scores. MobileNetV1 model 
employs depthwise separable convolution, which is used to 
process features efficiently after which fully connected layer 
is used to make the classification. The model separates actual 
and fake videos according to the facial features that were 
obtained on the basis of the frames. The accuracy, precision, 
recall, ROC AUC, and PR AUC measures are used to assess 
the performance of the model, and a confusion matrix is 
created to evaluate the performance of the classification and 
the Table II demonstrates that the training process. 
D.  Training Configuration 
The deep learning architectures were also trained in 
Jupyter Notebook on a local machine with the NVIDIA 
GeForce RTX 3060 (12 GB), Intel Core i7 (12th Gen) 
processor, and 64 GB of RAM. The binary classification 
between real and fake frames was trained on ImageNet-
pretrained weights on the Celeb-DF v2 dataset. Input images 
were normalized and 224 x 224 x 3 images were resized. The 
model has been trained on 50 epochs, which offers the trade-
off of performance and training time because additional 
epochs gained fewer benefits, but more computationally. 
Less epochs caused underfitting and more resulted in 
overfitting. The batch size of 16 was selected so that training 
can be as fast as possible with minimal memory usage and 
not to use high-level memory and slowness of training. 
Accordingly, the batch size of 16 and 50 epochs were 
considered the best to stabilize performance and effectively 
compute.

III. RESULT AND DISCUSSION  
In this section, we first discuss the performance of the 
proposed deepfake detection method and provide a 
comprehensive analysis of the obtained results. The 
experiments were carried out on the Celeb-DF v2 dataset, 
which is made up of high quality real and fake videos 
purposely to test the strength of the detection algorithms. In 
order to make a fair comparison, seven popular convolutional 
neural network (CNN) models, i.e. ResNet50, VGG16, 
VGG19, 
Xception, 
InceptionV3, 
MobileNetV2, 
and 
MobileNetV1, were trained and tested with the same 
xperimental condition. Various measures of performance 
were used in the evaluation, such as accuracy, precision, 
recall, loss, and Area Under the Curve (AUC), to present all 
the models in a comparison manner and these results are 
summarized in the following sections starting with a 
performance table, then discussions on training behavior in 
terms of accuracy and loss curves, the classification 
reliability in terms of confusion matrices and the 
discriminative ability in terms of ROC and precision-recall 
curves. Lastly, a comparative analysis is used to point out the 
most effective models and their implication to deepfake 
detection. 
Table III summarizes the performance of all the models 
and shows the most important model evaluation techniques, 
including accuracy, precision, recall, loss, ROC-AUC, and 
PR-AUC. As presented, MobileNetV1 was the most accurate 
with 0.9824 followed by ResNet50 at 0.9810. MobileNetV1 
was also a little bit better than ResNet50 in the loss (0.0535 
vs 0.0579) and also had a score of 1.000 on MobileNetV1 
ROC-AUC and PR-AUC. These findings also show that the 
MobileNetV1 are very effective in discriminating between 
real and fake content in the Celeb-DF v2 dataset. The 
accuracy of Xception was next, 0.9780 then VGG19 at 
0.9736. Nonetheless, VGG16 had the poorest performance as 
the accuracy stood at 0.9439 and loss at 0.2099, which 
implies that the simpler architecture of VGG16 was not 
coping with the difficulty of detecting deepfake.

Fig. 3 shows the training and validation accuracy curve of 
MobileNetV1, with a rapid and steady improvement with the 
50 epochs of training. At the end, the model had an accuracy 
of 98% and the validation accuracy was close to the training 
accuracy. These minimal variations during the initial stages 
show effective learning and the model learns rapidly the task. 
The performance kept increasing with the training and it was 
observed that MobileNetV1 was successfully learning to 
differentiate between fake and original content. The fact that 
the training curve and the validation curve were similar 
indicates that the model was not overfitting but rather it 
generalized. In order to address overfitting, the concept of 
transfer learning was utilized, in which the pre-trained 
weights of ImageNet were used to take advantage of features 
that had been previously learned, and early stopping was used 
to terminate training once the validation performance ceased 
to improve and stopped additional overfitting. This stable 
performance demonstrates that MobileNetV1 can be applied 
to more complex deepfake detection tasks with a high level 
of accuracy. 
Fig. 3.  Accuracy curve 
Fig 4, which shows the training and validation loss curves 
of MobileNetV1, depicts a steady and gradual reduction in 
the loss during the 50 epochs. Firstly, we can observe a 
significant decrease in training and validation losses during 
the beginning of the training, as well as a steep decline during 
the early epochs. Following this initial downward trend, the 
losses level off and steadily reduce, which means that the 
model is actually learning and gaining the ability to 
differentiate between real and fake content. The fact that the 
loss of training (blue line) and validation loss (red line) follow 
a similar pattern during the training process implies that the 
model is not underfitting since both training and validation 
loss converge at the same rate. This steady decrease in loss is 
an indication of the MobileNetV1 model being efficient in 
extracting complexity patterns of the data without overfitting 
or underfitting. The stabilization of the two loss curves means 
that the model has hit a convergence point and any further 
training would not achieve a major improvement in the 
performance of the model.

In TABLE IV, it is demonstrated that the proposed 
deepfake detection model based on MobileNetV1 performs 
better than a number of state-of-the-art approaches on the 
Celeb-DF v2 dataset. Existing models had an accuracy of 
83.75% to 97.7% with the MobileNetV1 achieve 98.24%, and 
a new high in detecting deepfakes. This proves that 
MobileNetV1 is a lightweight and efficient model, making it 
suitable for real-time deepfake detection, especially in 
resource-constrained environments.  
Despite the strong performance, there are some 
limitations. The model is primarily trained and tested on a 
single dataset, which restricts its ability to generalize across 
different deepfake. Additionally, the model may struggle 
with more sophisticated deepfakes that involve compression 
artifacts. The lack of explainability also points to the need for 
Explainable AI (XAI) to improve model transparency and 
trust. Future work will focus on cross-dataset generalization, 
enhancing adversarial robustness, and extending the model to 
video-level detection using temporal cues. Moreover, 
addressing the model's interpretability through XAI 
techniques will be essential for broader adoption in real-
world applications. 
IV. CONCLUSION 
This paper has provided a critical analysis of deepfake 
detection models on the Celeb-DF v2 dataset and compared 
the accuracy of seven commonly known CNN models, 
including 
ResNet50, 
VGG16, 
VGG19, 
Xception, 
InceptionV3, 
MobileNetV2 
and 
MobileNetV1. 
The 
outcomes showed that MobileNetV1 and ResNet50 were the 
most effective as the former reached an accuracy of 0.9824, 
and all ROC-AUC and PR-AUC scores were equal to 1.000, 
which indicates its effectiveness in deepfake detection. In 
addition, MobileNetV1 performed better in loss than 
ResNet50, which demonstrates that the MobileNetV1 can 
achieve a high performance with the ability to reduce error. 
Although other models like Xception and VGG19 performed 
well, they were ranked at the bottom compared to the top two 
models particularly in accuracy and loss.
