Abstract—This Deepfake technology has emerged as a 
powerful tool for generating synthetic media, leveraging 
advancements in artificial intelligence and deep learning to 
create highly realistic fake images and videos. While deepfakes 
offer potential benefits in fields such as entertainment, media 
production, and virtual reality, they also present serious 
threats, including misinformation, identity fraud, and privacy 
violations. The ability to detect deepfake content accurately 
and efficiently is critical in safeguarding digital authenticity 
and preventing misuse. This research focuses on the 
development of deepfake detection models using Convolutional 
Neural Networks (CNNs) and MobileNetV2. Three primary 
detection methods— handcrafted feature analysis, machine 
learning-based classification, and artifact detection—were 
implemented and evaluated. A dataset consisting of 20,000 
images, equally split between real and fake, was used for model 
training 
and 
testing, 
and 
optimized 
using 
dropout 
regularization, learning rate tuning, and early stopping. The 
CNN model achieved an accuracy of 90%, while an optimized 
MobileNetV2 Subtraction model improved detection accuracy 
to 97% by reducing overfitting and refining hyperparameters. 
The study underscores the importance of robust deepfake 
detection 
frameworks, 
particularly 
in 
an 
era 
where 
manipulated content is becoming increasingly difficult to 
differentiate from real media. Future enhancements will 
explore real-time detection solutions and adversarial training 
techniques to further improve detection accuracy and 
resilience.

I. INTRODUCTION  
Deepfake technology, a result of advancements in 
artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML), 
enables the creation of manipulated media that convincingly 
replicates real-life appearances and expressions. Leveraging 
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), deepfake 
systems generate highly realistic synthetic content by 
training neural networks to produce and refine forged media 
iteratively. The increasing accessibility of deepfake 
generation tools has escalated these risks, necessitating the 
development of robust detection mechanisms. Traditional 
methods for detecting manipulated content, such as forensic 
analysis and manual inspection, prove inadequate against 
sophisticated AI-generated media.  
This paper explores deepfake detection using CNNs, 
focusing on three primary techniques: (i) handcrafted feature 
analysis, 
which 
identifies 
inconsistencies 
in 
facial 
expressions, (ii) machine learning-based classification, 
which distinguishes between real and fake media based on 
pixel-level analysis, and (iii) artifact detection, which 
identifies GAN-generated traces in manipulated images. 
This study focuses on image-based detection due to its low 
computational overhead and suitability for real-time 
deployment. In contrast, video-based methods analyze 
temporal inconsistencies but often require more resources. 
The primary objectives of this study are:  
• 
To develop a CNN-based deepfake detection 
framework. 
• 
To assess the efficiency of MobileNetV2 in real-
time detection. 
• 
To enhance accuracy through an optimized 
MobileNetV2 Subtraction model.

III. METHODOLOGY   
This study aimed to develop and evaluate machine 
learning models for detecting deepfake images, with a 
particular emphasis on the use of different neural network 
architectures, data preprocessing techniques, and artifact 
analysis for robust detection. Below is a detailed description 
of the dataset, preprocessing steps, model selection, training 
and optimization techniques, feature extraction, evaluation 
metrics, and potential deployment scenarios.  
A. Dataset and Preprocessing  
The dataset used in this study consists of 20,000 labeled 
images, with an equal distribution of real and deepfake 
generated images. The dataset includes samples from 
FaceForensics++, Celeb-DF, and GAN-generated images 
using StyleGAN. The choice of dataset was to ensure a 
comprehensive representation of both authentic and 
manipulated images, allowing the models to distinguish 
between the two effectively  
First, Faces were aligned and cropped using MTCNN 
before resizing to 96×96. Resizing ensures that the models 
could process all images in the same format, reducing the 
complexity of input variation that could arise from different 
image sizes. To normalize the dataset, pixel values were 
scaled to a range of [0, 1], preventing any inconsistency that 
might arise due to variations in brightness or contrast across 
images. Image normalization is an essential step in deep 
learning models to maintain uniformity in the input data, thus 
improving model convergence.  
Further, data augmentation was applied to enhance the 
model’s ability to generalize and perform well across a wide 
variety of images. Augmentation techniques included 
rotation, flipping, and zooming, which simulated variations 
in facial features and distortions caused by image 
manipulation. By applying these transformations, the models 
were trained to identify deepfake artifacts under different 
conditions, enhancing their robustness and generalization 
capabilities.  
The dataset was then split into three subsets: training 
(80%), validation (10%), and testing (10%). Stratified 
sampling was employed to ensure the distribution of real and 
fake images was proportionally represented in each subset. 
This method helped preserve class balance, which is critical 
in training deep learning models for classification tasks. The 
Image Data Generator utility from Keras was used for real-
time data augmentation during the training phase, ensuring 
that each batch of images presented to the model was 
different, thereby reducing the likelihood of overfitting.  
B. Model Selection and Architecture  
Three distinct neural network architectures were selected 
and implemented for deepfake detection: a Convolutional 
Neural Network (CNN), MobileNetV2, and a modified 
version of MobileNetV2, referred to as MobileNetV2 
Subtraction. These models were chosen to represent a range 
of approaches, from a traditional CNN baseline to more 
efficient, lightweight models.  
1) Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)  
CNN served as the baseline model in this study. 
CNNs are well-suited for image classification tasks as 
they are able to extract spatial hierarchies of features from 
images. The architecture in figure 1 consisted of multiple 
convolutional layers followed by ReLU activation 
functions, which helped capture complex patterns in the 
input images. Max-pooling layers were used after each 
convolution to down sample the image and retain only the 
most important features. The final layers of the model 
included fully connected (dense) layers, along with 
dropout mechanisms to prevent overfitting.  
Fig: 1 Architecture of the CNN model used for deepfake detection. 
2) MobileNetV2   
 
MobileNetV2 is a more lightweight architecture that 
employs depth-wise separable convolutions, which reduces 
computational cost while maintaining accuracy. This model 
was chosen for its efficiency, particularly in scenarios 
requiring real-time processing, such as mobile devices and 
social media platforms. Pre-trained weights from ImageNet 
were used in the initial layers of the MobileNetV2 model, 
and fine-tuning was performed to adapt the model

3) MobileNetV2 Subtraction  
The MobileNetV2 Subtraction model was an optimized 
version of the previous model. This iteration focused on 
mitigating overfitting by reducing the training dataset size 
and limiting the number of training epochs. Hyperparameter 
tuning played a significant role in refining this model, 
including adjusting the batch size, learning rates, and 
dropout rates. This model demonstrated the highest 
accuracy (97%) among the architectures tested, as it 
effectively focused on identifying subtle deepfake artifacts. 
The MobileNetV2 Subtraction model introduces a dual-
input pipeline where two feature maps (original and 
perturbed) undergo pixel-wise subtraction. This highlights 
subtle discrepancies indicative of deepfake manipulations, 
provided an efficient solution without compromising 
computational performance, making it suitable for real-time 
applications. 
C. Training and Optimization  
All models were trained using binary cross-entropy loss, 
a common loss function for binary classification tasks. The 
Adam optimizer was chosen for its adaptive learning rate and 
its ability to converge quickly. The initial learning rate was 
set to 0.001, and training was conducted over 50 epochs, with 
early stopping mechanisms implemented to prevent 
overfitting.  
During training, performance was closely monitored 
using several key metrics: precision, recall, F1-score, and 
accuracy. These metrics offered insights into the 
effectiveness of the models in distinguishing real images 
from deepfakes, with precision and recall helping to balance 
false positives and false negatives. All models were trained 
on an NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU, with a batch size of 32 and 
binary cross-entropy loss. The model achieved 97% 
accuracy on the test set, with ±0.8% confidence over five 
runs. Accuracy provided an overall measure of correct 
classifications, while the F1-score provided a balance 
between precision and recall.  
D. Feature Extraction and Artifact Analysis  
In addition to the deep learning models, this study 
explored feature extraction and artifact analysis techniques 
to enhance detection accuracy. By analyzing specific 
artifacts that are unique to deepfake images, the models 
could gain additional insights into the manipulation process 
i. 
Fourier Transform Analysis: This technique was 
used to detect frequency discrepancies introduced 
by generative adversarial networks (GANs). 
Deepfakes often exhibit unusual frequency patterns 
that can be detected through Fourier transforms, 
highlighting potential image manipulations. 
ii. 
PRNU (Photo Response Non-Uniformity) Analysis: 
PRNU analysis examines inconsistencies in sensor 
pattern noise between real and fake images. Real 
images often have unique noise patterns, while 
deepfake images might not align with these natural 
noise patterns, offering a clue to the image's 
authenticity 
iii. 
Edge Detection Techniques: Deepfake images often 
suffer from unnatural blurring or boundary artifacts, 
which can be detected using edge detection 
techniques.  
By combining deep learning models with artifact 
analysis, this study created a more comprehensive 
framework for deepfake detection. The fusion of CNN-based 
detection with additional artifact analysis provided a multi-
modal approach that improved overall model accuracy and 
robustness.  
E. Evaluation Metrics  
To assess the performance of each model, a variety of 
evaluation metrics were considered:  
• 
Accuracy: The proportion of correctly classified 
images.  
• 
Precision and Recall: Precision measures the 
model’s ability to correctly identify deepfake 
images, while recalling measures how well the 
model detects all real deepfakes.  
• 
F1-Score: The harmonic means of precision and 
recall, providing a balanced measure of model 
performance.

V. SIMULATION RESULTS 
Fig 3: Dataset 1 
       The Deepfake detection project delivered impressive 
results across the models it tested—CNN (Fig 1), 
MobileNetV2, and the MobileNetV2 Subtraction model. 
Among them, the CNN model stood out, reaching an overall 
accuracy of 90%. It consistently identified both real and fake 
images with a high success rate. The model’s strength lay in 
its ability to extract distinct features through its 
convolutional layers, making it particularly effective at 
detecting manipulated media. That said, the model faced 
challenges when it came to compressed images. The 97% 
accuracy was achieved on an unseen, stratified test set 
containing 2,000 images (50% real, 50% fake. As a result, 
the CNN’s performance dropped when dealing with lower-
quality or heavily compressed content, limiting its ability to 
spot fakes with the same precision. Despite this, the CNN 
model remains a strong contender in the fight against 
deepfake content, thanks to its ability to analyze intricate 
visual patterns and separate genuine images from those that 
have been altered as illustrated in Fig. 4. 
Fig 4: Dataset 2 
       The 
initial 
MobileNetV2 
model, 
designed 
for 
lightweight computation, faced difficulties, reaching only 
50% accuracy (Figure 5). The base MobileNetV2 struggled 
due to lack of fine-tuning and weak generalization from 
ImageNet-pretrained features. Its limitations stemmed from 
overfitting and sensitivity to dataset variations, highlighting 
issues with using a pre-trained model on new, varied datasets. 
MobileNetV2’s focus on efficiency over accuracy showed its 
limitations in distinguishing subtle details between real and 
fake images, especially in compressed or lower-quality 
formats. This underscored the need for modifications to 
improve its detection capability. 
Fig 5: Accuracy curve of MobileNetV2 model 
The optimized MobileNetV2 Subtraction model, however, 
achieved impressive results, reaching a 97% accuracy rate. 
By reducing the dataset and limiting the number of training 
epochs, the model avoided overfitting, allowing it to 
generalize better and detect fake images across different 
quality levels, including compressed media. This optimized 
model demonstrated high detection effectiveness, indicating 
that streamlined, lightweight models, when fine-tuned, can 
successfully deliver high detection accuracy while 
remaining computationally efficient. Overall, the project 
demonstrated that combining efficient model structures with 
careful tuning can yield robust Deepfake detection, with 
particular promise for mobile and real-time applications.

VII. CONCLUSION  
       The project successfully developed a deep learning 
model to detect deepfake images using convolutional neural 
networks (CNNs). Through rigorous training and testing, 
the model achieved high accuracy and robust performance 
metrics. The use of CNNs allowed for efficient feature 
extraction, capturing subtle inconsistencies in facial 
movements and textures often present in manipulated 
videos. The model's effectiveness was validated across 
diverse test datasets, indicating its generalizability and 
reliability in identifying deepfakes. Overall, this project 
contributes to the ongoing battle against misinformation by 
providing an automated and accurate tool for deepfake 
detection. Future work includes adversarial training, cross-
modal analysis (audio/video), and deployment in real-time 
verification tools.

VIII. FUTURE WORK 
       The future scope of this deepfake detection project is 
broad, given the increasing prevalence of deepfake 
technology and its potential misuse. One promising direction 
is enhancing the model by integrating additional machine 
learning techniques, such as ensemble learning or hybrid 
approaches that combine CNNs with recurrent neural 
networks (RNNs) or transformers. Another area for 
advancement is real-time processing. As the model is further 
optimized, it could be adapted for deployment in live video 
streams to detect deepfakes in real-time, which would be 
highly beneficial for social media platforms, news agencies, 
and video conferencing services. Additionally, using 
adversarial training with Generative Adversarial Networks 
(GANs) could improve the model's resilience against 
advanced deepfake generation techniques. By integrating this 
deepfake detection model with verification systems, 
organizations could enhance their capacity to combat 
misinformation. Lastly, the ethical and regulatory landscape 
surrounding deepfakes is evolving, so this project could 
contribute valuable insights into establishing standards and 
guidelines for deepfake identification, verification, and 
responsible AI use. In summary, this project lays the 
groundwork for a more robust and adaptable system, capable 
of addressing future challenges in the field of deepfake 
detection.
