AbstractDeep fake content, especially in images and 
videos, is spreading at an unprecedented rate.  Fake content 
are generated with the help of advanced deep learning (DL) 
algorithms such as GANs, autoencoders, and variation 
autoencoders. This fake content spreads misinformation, 
causing a severe impact on society by degrading the 
trustworthiness of content on social media. Mitigating the risk 
of these techniques can be done by utilizing the power of one of 
the DL models, which is CNN. This research study focuses on 
analysing various Deep-Fake (DF) identification techniques 
that are trained on various datasets with a small number of 
samples. The proposed work demonstrates an efficient CNN 
model and three other CNN pre-trained models through 
transfer learning on a large dataset available on Kaggle, 
consisting of 140k images of faces. The proposed CNN model 
achieved a high accuracy of 96%, while DenseNet121 reached 
97%. Both EfficientNetB0 and MobileNet demonstrated even 
higher performance, each achieving an accuracy of 98% 
within 15 epochs.

I. INTRODUCTION
Nowadays, technology is available to everyone, and 
people are being exposed to the latest advancements. 
Recent times have witnessed a drastic technological 
advancement in artificial intelligence making machines to 
think and act according to the instructions given to them. 
The development of Deep Fakes (DFs) employ deep 
learning algorithms to produce realistic illustration but 
artificial media without the knowledge of the people 
portrayed, has been done by using AI based image and 
video editing tools. These algorithms can replicate human 
behavior and speech by utilizing open-source deep learning 
frameworks and the wealth of online material. This presents 
a huge challenge in the fight against the spread of fake 
content. 
 
Deep Fake (DF) technology has both positive and 
negative aspects. On the positive side, it can generate 
morphed content for the entertainment industry, such as de-
aging actors or bringing deceased ones back, as well as for 
voice acting, dubbing, and creating realistic training content 
for doctors. It can help teach computers to spot fake content, 
recreate historical events, and enhance old audio and video 
recordings. However, the misuse of DF technology poses 
significant risks, such as spreading misinformation through 
fake videos of public figures, impacting elections and 
public trust, enabling phishing attacks, and creating non-
consensual adult content. Detecting and stopping fake 
content remains challenging, necessitating better methods to 
distinguish real from fake and educating people on 
recognizing the differences. This work focuses on DFs, 
which use DL, a type of artificial intelligence that employs 
multi-layer artificial neural networks to imitate the 
functioning of the human brain. DL algorithms extract 
complex information from data by processing it gradually. 
To be able to recognize complex patterns, these models 
must be trained on large datasets. DL helps AI understand 
complex facial emotions, mouth movements, and voice 
patterns in source footage and high-resolution photos, 
which helps AI create convincing DFs. These algorithms 
mimic the target person's speech patterns, realistic emotions, 
and facial features with great accuracy.  
 
Some DL techniques to generate DFs are described 
below. 
 
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) generate 
artificial images or videos using a two-player game 
approach: the generator creates realistic synthetic data, 
while the discriminator evaluates its authenticity. The 
generator iteratively improves based on the discriminator's 
feedback until the synthetic data is indistinguishable from 
real data. In DeepFakes, this technique manipulates media 
to convincingly alter faces or voices.[13] [10] [8]. 
Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) and Autoencoders are 
both types of DL neural networks used for compressing and 
reconstructing data. VAEs focus on capturing the essence of 
the original data in a smaller space, while in DFs, VAEs can 
be employed to encode both the original and target faces, 
allowing for subtle changes. Autoencoders, on the other 
hand, learn from large data to make realistic faces or voices, 
even when there are limited samples, and are also used to 
enhance image compression by generating compact 
versions of images [12]. Both are crucial for creating 
effective DFs. 
Additionaly Facial Recognition Algorithms are used to 
identify and track people's faces in images or videos. This 
helps in selecting the target face for manipulation in DFs [7].

II. LITERATURE REVIEW 
Detecting DF is not a new paradigm; several research 
studies have been conducted on DF detection in both 
images and videos. Many works have been published on 
this topic. Mostly, the data used to train these models were 
generated by GANs because these networks have the ability 
to automate the generation of DF images, which are 
difficult for human eyes to detect. However, there are 
several other techniques present to detect the images 
generated by GANs. One such technique is using CNN for 
classifying whether an image is real or fake, as CNNs are 
meant to classify images. Many authors have employed 
CNN architecture to automatically learn hierarchical 
representations of features from raw image pixels. 
Several research studies have focused on detecting DF 
face images using CNN models as a means to detect fake 
images. One such paper is [1], the authors aim to utilize the 
effectiveness of CNN models to detect fake images. The 
aims to achieve higher accuracy in classifying fake images, 
mainly by reducing the cost and effort of detection. The 
dataset of 9000 images undergoes data augmentation, and 
fed into their custom CNN model, which is a 13-layer 
architecture, alongside some pre-trained models are used. 
The ResNet50 model performed the best with 99.18% 
accuracy. Their custom CNN model achieved 95.21% 
accuracy. Other models like MobileNet and InceptionV3 
also showed over 98% accuracy.  
In [2], the authors used the “real-and-fake-face-
detection-dataset” from Kaggle, pre-processed with data 
augmentation. They employed a custom CNN model with 4 
Conv2D, 4 Maxpooling, and ReLU activation layers, 
achieving 98.46% training and 99.44% testing accuracy 
after 150 epochs. To address limitations in [2] regarding 
video detection, the authors proposed [3], where they used 
the same CNN model and tested on videos instead of 
images. They applied the Haar Cascade method to extract 
facial regions from video frames, and if any frame was 
predicted as fake, the entire video was classified as fake. 
Their method correctly identified 91 out of 100 videos from 
the DF Detection Challenge dataset.  
The custom CNN model in [4] consists of 5 
convolutional blocks. This paper uses Flickr’s 140k dataset 
which is present in Kaggle is used to train and validate the 
model. Data augmentation was applied on the dataset. The 
dataset consists of color images with 3 channels which were 
converted to grayscale with just 1 channel to simplify the 
training process, the proposed CNN model achieved an 
accuracy of 91.82%, F1 score of 91% and a precision is 
81% in detecting deep fake faces.  
The paper [5] the authors have utilized the potential of 
pre-trained models to detect fake images to that of real 
once, firstly they have applied Error Level Analysis to the 
images in their dataset which is a technique that is used to 
identify whether an image has been modified or not based 
on the JPEG compression levels. The ELA images were 
sent to those pre-trained models for training, the extracted 
features were then classified using SVM and KNN with 
hyperparameter optimization. The highest accuracy of 
89.5% was achieved using the ResNet18 architecture and 
the KNN classifier with optimized hyper parameters. 
GoogLeNet achieved an accuracy of 81% with KNN, while 
SqueezeNet obtained 69.4% accuracy with SVM.  
The custom CNN model in [6] is trained detect the DF 
videos. The authors have created a dataset from videos 
these images were then pre-processed by using data 
augmentation, then sent to their custom model for training, 
various optimization functions and loss functions were used 
for training, then the model was run on testing and 
validation data. To improve accuracy, additional parameter 
and hyperparameter tuning was done. Finally, the model 
was run on a sample video, and classes were identified in 
each frame of the video. 
The authors of study [7] used a layered approach, first 
identifying the person's face in the picture using already-
existing facial recognition networks. After that, CNN is 
used to extract face features, which are subsequently fed to 
LSTM model to examine facial modifications and temporal 
sequences between frames. The last stage is a Recycle-
GAN, which combines spatial and temporal data using

The paper focuses on developing a DF detection system 
to fight against the wide-spread of manipulated media 
content. The study uses several models and techniques to 
tackle the increasing worries about whether digital content 
is real and trustworthy. Beginning with data preprocessing, 
the proposed system applies data augmentation techniques 
to enhance diversity and robustness. The model definition 
stage plays a crucial role, with a focus on designing a DL 
architecture optimized for effective feature extraction and 
classification, the model is designed to detect minor 
irregularities that suggest the content has been artificially 
changed or modified.  
The first step in the proposed system involves the 
acquisition of data from the “DF” dataset, which contains 
Real and Fake images. The dataset (‘deepfake-and-real-
images’) under consideration consists of a total of 70k x 2

images are 256x256 JPG files and are intended to be used 
in the DF Detection Challenge on Kaggle. These images 
are structured into two directories, Fake and Real images. The 
dataset is designed for the purpose of DF detection.

i. Entry Block 
The model starts with an entry block, which serves as 
the initial processing stage for the input image. In this block 
The input image, represented as “(180, 180, 3)” for RGB 
channels, is convolved with 128 filters of size 3x3 with a 
stride of 2. This initial convolution reduces the spatial 
dimensions of the image. The output of the Conv2D layer is 
then normalized using batch normalization. This step helps 
in stabilizing and accelerating the training process by 
normalizing the activations. Then a Rectified Linear Unit 
(ReLU) activation function is applied. ReLU introduces 
non-linearity to the network, allowing it to learn complex 
patterns in the data.

ii. Multiple Blocks 
The model consists of multiple blocks, used for feature 
extraction and representation. These blocks are repeated for 
sizes `[256, 512, 728]` , allowing it to learn features at 
different scales and complexities. These values represent 
the number of filters used in each depthwise separable 
convolutional layer within each block. Different sizes allow 
the model to learn features at different scales and 
complexities. This technique is often used in deep neural 
networks architecture to improve the model's ability to 
capture and represent various levels of information in the 
input data. 
 
Components of Each Block 
1. Activation (ReLU) 
After each block's input, another ReLU (Rectified 
Linear Unit) activation is applied. It is a non-linear 
activation function that introduces non-linearity to the 
network. It helps in learning complex patterns and 
features in the data, allowing the network to model more 
complicated relationships. 
 
2. Depthwise Separable Convolution 
Two depthwise separable convolutional layers are 
stacked together in each block. These layers are designed 
to be computationally efficient, reducing the number of 
parameters and allow the model to learn spatial and 
cross-channel features effectively, it consists of the two 
layers 2.1 and 2.2. 
   
2.1. Depthwise Convolution 
The first part of the layer is a depthwise convolution, 
where each input channel is convolved separately with its 
own kernel. This operation applies a 3x3 convolution to 
each channel of the input independently. The goal is to 
learn spatial features within each channel. 
 
2.2. Pointwise Convolution 
The second part of the layer is a pointwise 
convolution, which combines the output of the depthwise 
convolution. It uses 1x1 convolutions to mix information 
from different channels. This step helps in increasing the 
model's capacity to learn complex patterns and 
relationships across channels. 
 
1. Batch Normalization (BN) 
Batch normalization is applied after every depthwise 
separable convolutional layer. It is used to normalize the 
output of the previous layer across the batch dimension. 
It helps in stabilizing and accelerating the training 
process. By normalizing the activations, it makes sure 
that the model can train faster and do not overfit. The 
first BN layer occurs after the first depthwise separable 
convolution and the second one after the second 
depthwise separable convolution. 
 
2. MaxPooling2D 
After the two depthwise separable convolutions and 
BN, MaxPooling2D layer is applied. It downsamples the 
feature maps by taking the maximum value from a 3x3 
window and moving with a stride of 2. It helps in finding 
the most important features and reducing the spatial 
resolution. 
 
Each block in the model contains two depthwise 
separable convolutional layers then batch normalization and 
ReLU activation, all of these are important for feature 
extraction. 
 
Residual Connections 
In each block, a residual connection is introduced to 
help in training the network more effectively. It adds the 
output of the current block to a Conv2D layer's output from 
the previous block. This helps in reusing the important 
information again without being vanished, so helps stopping 
the vanishing gradient problem. The residual connection is 
created after the MaxPooling2D layer. 
 
Within each iteration, the "current block" consists of 
two Depthwise Separable Convolutional layers, BN after 
each layer, ReLU activation after each BN, and finally the 
MaxPooling2D layer. The output from this "current block" 
is then combined with the residual connection from the 
Conv2D layer of the previous block to form the output for 
that iteration. This structure helps in learning residual 
functions, which can be beneficial for training deep neural 
networks. 
 
iii. Final Layers 
Towards the end of the model, after repeated blocks, the 
following layers are applied. A final depthwise separable 
convolutional layer with 1024 filters and a 3x3 kernel is 
applied. This layer further refines the learned features. BN 
is applied again, followed by ReLU activation.Then Global 
Average Pooling layer averages the feature maps across 
spatial dimensions, reducing each feature map to a single 
value. It helps in capturing the most important information 
from the feature maps. To prevent overfitting, a dropout 
layer with a dropout rate of 0.25 is included. Dropout helps 
in generalization. The final layer is a dense layer that 
produces the model's output. The model is used for binary 
classification; it has one unit with no activation. In our case,

the

ii. EfficientNetB0 (CNN Model - 3) 
EfficientNetB0, developed by Google researchers, 
balances 
complexity 
and 
efficiency 
for 
superior

image classification and other computer vision tasks in

efficiency make it a popular choice for real-time 
applications such as DF detection. 
iii. DenseNet121 (CNN Model - 4) 
DenseNet121, developed by Facebook AI Research 
(FAIR), features a dense connectivity structure where each 
layer passes data to all subsequent layers within its block, 
promoting feature reuse and mitigating the vanishing 
gradient problem. Comprising dense blocks and transition 
layers, it manages parameter growth and maintains spatial 
dimensions of feature maps, achieving cutting-edge 
performance in image classification without excessive 
complexity. DenseNet121's efficient use of parameters and 
ability to learn deep features with fewer parameters make it 
computationally 
efficient 
and 
versatile 
for 
various 
applications, including impressive performance in DF 
detection.

V. CONCLUSION 
The Deep Fake detection system implemented in this 
paper has shown better performance in distinguishing real 
images from fake ones. This research study has considered 
four CNN models of which one is the proposed CNN model 
and rest are pre-trained models, trained on dataset 
containing images. This research study has utilized the 
power of pre-trained models by unfreezing all of its layers, 
which helps the proposed model to learn specific features in 
the considered dataset. The proposed model has achieved a 
good accuracy when trained on large dataset in less number 
of epochs.
