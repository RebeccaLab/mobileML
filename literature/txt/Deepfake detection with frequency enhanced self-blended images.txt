A B S T R A C T
Advances in deepfake research have led to the creation of almost perfect image manipulations that are
undetectable to the human eye and some deepfake detection tools. Recently, several techniques have been
proposed to differentiate deepfakes from real images and videos. This study introduces a frequency enhanced
self-blended images (FSBI) approach for deepfake detection. This proposed approach utilizes discrete wavelet
transforms (DWT) to extract discriminative features from self-blended images (SBI). The features are then used
to train a convolutional network architecture model. SBIs blend the image with itself by introducing several
forgery artifacts in a copy of the image before blending it. This prevents the classifier from overfitting specific
artifacts by learning more generic representations. These blended images are then fed into the frequency
feature extractor to detect artifacts that could not be detected easily in the time domain. The proposed
approach was evaluated on FF++ and Celeb-DF datasets, and the obtained results outperformed state-of-the-
art techniques using the cross-dataset evaluation protocol, achieving an AUC of 95.49% on Celeb-DF dataset.
It also achieved competitive performance in the within-dataset evaluation setup. These results highlight the
robustness and effectiveness of our method in addressing the challenging generalization problem inherent in
deepfake detection. The code is available at https://github.com/gufranSabri/FSBI.
1. Introduction
The rise of deepfake technology has introduced a new era of digital
manipulation, posing severe threats to the authenticity and integrity
of multimedia content. Deepfakes, which are artificially generated me-
dia convincingly mimicking real individuals or events, present several
critical challenges. One major concern is their potential to spread
misinformation on an unprecedented scale, undermining public trust
in digital content and traditional media. They can be weaponized
to create fake news, manipulate public opinion, and interfere with
political processes, such as elections or policy decisions. Deepfakes
also enable malicious content creation, such as non-consensual explicit
videos or fraudulent communications, which can lead to reputational
damage, emotional distress, and legal complications for victims. More-
over, deepfake technology threatens privacy and security by facilitating
identity theft and impersonation in financial fraud or cyberattacks.
These multifaceted challenges underscore the urgent need for robust
detection methods to mitigate the risks posed by this rapidly evolving
technology.
Several techniques have been proposed in the literature for generat-
ing fake images that can be synthesized entirely or partially. Artificial
neural networks [1], auto-encoding variations [2,3], and generative ad-
versarial networks (GAN) [4] play significant roles in the development

The detection of deceptive digital creation is a complex and press-
ing challenge. This complexity arises from the rapid advancements in
generative models, which produce content that is increasingly indistin-

make it difficult to develop universally effective detection strategies.
Traditional methods for identifying deepfakes have become increas-

robust deepfake detection mechanisms are imperative for safeguarding
the credibility of digital media and maintaining public trust.
In the dynamic field of deepfake detection, diverse techniques
that combine traditional methods with cutting-edge deep learning
approaches have emerged. Traditional techniques depend on the exam-

to detect inconsistencies. In addition, visual facial artifacts such as eyes

self-blended images (SBIs) approach. The pseudo-source and target

transformations. These images were blended to create SBIs used for
deepfake detection. This study proposes an enhancement to the ap-
proach proposed in et al. [23]. We propose a frequency-enhanced SBIs
(FSBIs) approach for deepfake detection. This approach extends the SBI
method by extracting features from the SBIs in the frequency domain
to develop a more generic and robust detector. Each channel of the
blended images resulting from the SBI was fed into discrete wavelet
transforms (DWT). The resulting features were combined and fed into
a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model to learn the artifacts in
deepfake images. We used within-dataset and cross-dataset evaluation
protocols to evaluate the proposed approach and new state-of-the-
art results were obtained using the FF++ and Celeb-DF datasets. The
proposed approach showed a good capability for detecting the artifacts
on the deepfake images, as shown in Fig. 1. This study makes the
following contributions:
• Proposing an innovative approach for deepfake detection.
• Discussing the efficiency of frequency-based transforms for detect-
ing artifacts on deepfake images.
• Evaluating the generalization of the proposed approach by per-
forming cross-dataset evaluation.
• Reporting a new state-of-the-art performance in the Celeb-DF
dataset.

2. Related works
Several techniques have been proposed for deepfake detection.
These techniques vary from simple computer vision algorithms to
complex deep-learning neural networks. Therefore, we classified these
techniques into traditional and deep-learning approaches.
Traditional Techniques. These techniques depend mainly on classic
computer vision operations for artifact detection. Although the term
‘‘traditional techniques’’ may suggest that these techniques are out-
dated, they are still being used for deepfake detection. Jeong et al. [24]
employed bilateral high-pass filters to detect amplification effects on
fake images. Similarly, Liu et al. proposed by a deepfake detector that
utilizes temporal identity inconsistencies between human faces in video
frames [25]. Although these techniques accurately detect deepfakes,
significant advances in deepfake generation have motivated researchers
to search for alternative deep learning techniques [26].
Deep Learning Techniques. Most recent deepfake detection tech-
niques depend on neural networks [27]. The majority of these tech-
niques [28–33] utilize CNNs for learning and classifying images into
real and fake classes. Most of these CNN models do not undergo end-
to-end training; instead, they rely on pre-trained models for feature
extraction. EfficientNet models are the dominant pre-trained models
used in most CNN-based deepfake detection techniques [34–36]. To
boost the accuracy of these techniques, several researchers have utilized
CNNs with transformer models [23,34–38]. Wodajo et al. [38] used a
CNN to extract spatial features and fed them into a vision transformer
(ViT) model that used an attention mechanism to categorize them
into real or fake images. Similarly, CNNs have been combined with
ViT by other researchers [35–37,39] and good results were obtained.
Chen et al. [40] proposed a face forgery detection method that learns
local features and detects inconsistencies. To address the generalizabil-
ity of current deepfake detection methods, Chen et al. [41] employed
a self-supervised learning-based method. The model employs a pool of
forgery configurations and synthesized augmented forgeries to detect
forgeries in images and videos. Lee et al. [42] presented a digital
forensic tool for detecting different types of deepfakes. The proposed
tool learns features using an AE-based model.
The generalization of deepfake detection techniques is essential for
the development of real-time systems. Therefore, researchers typically
perform cross-dataset evaluations with their proposed deepfake de-
tection approaches [5,29–32,35,43]. In this setting, the technique is
evaluated on a dataset different from that used for model training. For
example, Coccomini et al. [35] performed cross-forgery analysis using
ViT to detect deepfakes in various datasets. Luo et al. [30] used high-
frequency features and CNNs to generalize the detection methods. The
MCX-API [31] method employs pairwise learning to improve model
generalization. Deepfake Disrupter [43] was used with the help of hu-
man input and deepfake detection tools to identify deepfakes. However,
most proposed techniques cannot detect deepfakes that are not present
in the training model [26].
Recent deep learning advancements in deepfake detection have fo-
cused on tackling spatio-temporal inconsistencies and dynamic motion

3. Frequency enhanced self-blended images (FSBI)
We propose an FSBI approach for deepfake detection, and its frame-
work is given in Fig. 2. The proposed approach comprises three mod-
ules: an SBI generator, a frequency feature generator (FFG), and a CNN
classifier. The original image is fed into the SBI generator component
to create a blended image with artifacts. The FFG applies a series of
DWT wavelets to the resulting blended image to extract features in
the frequency domain. The features are then fused and fed into a CNN
classifier trained with an EfficientNet-B5 pre-trained model to learn and
classify images as real or fake.
3.1. Self-blended image (SBI)
SBIs are generated when an image is blended with itself. It was
proposed by Shiohara et al. and creates deepfakes by blending a face
with its mask, which is generated by applying several transformations
SBI [23]. The SBI method uses three primary stages to generate deep-
fakes from an original image. First, a source-target generator (STG)
component takes an image as an input and generates pseudo-source
(𝐼𝑠) and target (𝐼𝑡) images. These images are generated by performing
random image transformations to the original image. These transfor-
mations involve manipulating the values of RGB and HSV channels and
changing the brightness and contrast of the image. Additional augmen-
tation operations involve downsampling and translation is applied to
the source image to create landmark mismatches.
In the second stage, the SBI uses a mask generator (MG) to create a
gray-scale mask image that blends both the source and target images.
The MG generates a mask by detecting the landmarks of the facial
region and calculating the convex hull from these detected landmarks.
For this process, the SBI method adopts the FaceXray BI [50] to perform
landmark detection followed by elastic deformation [31]. The masks
are smoothed twice using two Gaussian filters and eroded after the first
filter is applied.
Finally, the SBI blends the source and target images using the
generated mask. The blended image is created by eroding the source
image with the mask generated by the MG module. The resulting
eroded image is then combined with the eroded target image, using
the inverse of the mask, to produce the final SBI. The steps were as
follows:

3.2. Frequency features generator (FFG)
The blended image obtained from the SBI module was fed into the
FFG module. This module leverages DWT to extract more discrimina-
tive features from blended images, aiding in the detection of artifacts
in deepfake images. DWT is a signal- and image-processing technique
that decomposes a signal into different frequency components by
iteratively applying filters to the signal, dividing it into approximate
and detailed coefficients at various resolutions. This process provides
multiresolution analysis that captures both global and local features
within the image.
DWT families, such as Haar, Daubechies, Symlet, Biorthogonal, and
Coiflet, offer different properties suitable for various applications. Haar
is computationally efficient but does not perform well for complex
structures. Daubechies, with varying orders, are effective for fine de-
tails and compression. Symlet balances smoothness and oscillations,
making it suitable for signals with both smooth and abrupt changes.
Biorthogonal offers flexibility with separate sets for decomposition and
reconstruction, while Coiflet, which has improved regularity, is suitable
for applications requiring smoother wavelets. The choice between these
families depends on the specific characteristics of the deepfake images
and the requirements of the detection task. In this study, we evalu-
ated the Haar, Symlet, and Biorthogonal wavelet types for deepfake
detection and obtained the best results using the Symlet wavelet type.
Additionally, several signal extension modes can be applied to
reduce artifacts resulting from signal extrapolation when computing
the DWT. These extension modes include symmetric, reflective, and

The RGB channels of the SBI were decomposed into three channels
(R, G, and B), and the DWT coefficient was computed for each channel.
The computed approximate coefficient for each channel was resized
to match the original images and averaged with the corresponding
SBI channel. The resulting coefficients were concatenated depthwise
to create a three-channel DWT image, referred to as FSBI. This image
highlights all the possible artifacts introduced into the deepfake image
during its generation.
3.3. CNN model
The final component of the proposed FSBI approach is a CNN
model trained to detect artifacts in the FSBIs and classify images as
real or fake. This model was trained on FSBIs, which encode the
SBI using frequency features extracted from each channel. We fine-
tuned the EfficientNet-B5 [51] pre-trained on ImageNet, for this task.
EfficientNet-B5, a member of the EfficientNet family, features com-
pound scaling, where width, depth, and resolution are uniformly scaled
to balance model capacity and computational cost. This design pri-
oritizes state-of-the-art accuracy in image classification tasks, such as
those on ImageNet, while using fewer parameters compared to other
models with similar performance.
4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental setup
Datasets. We employed two prominent datasets to evaluate the pro-
posed approach: FF++ [52] and Celeb-DF [53]. The FF++ dataset
consists of 1,000 videos with approximately 500,000 frames. This
dataset has fake images created using deepfake (DF) [54], face swap
(FS) [55], neural textures (NT) [56], and Face2Face (F2F) [57] meth-
ods. From the 1,000 real videos, FF++ constructed over 1.8 million
images derived from 4,000 fake videos. Due to the large size of the
FF++ dataset, we used the compressed c23 version of the FF++ dataset
(FF++(LQ)) in this work. We also used the Celeb-DF (version 2) dataset,
which comprises large-scale deepfake videos generated using special-
ized synthesis methods. The dataset comprises 5,639 high-quality deep-
fake videos featuring celebrities, which amount to approximately two
million frames.
Implementation Details. The model was trained and evaluated using
an Ubuntu 22.04 LTS machine equipped with an NVIDIA RTX A5000
GPU with 48 GB of memory. The PyTorch framework was used to im-
plement the proposed approach. The proposed model was trained using
an early stopping technique to avoid overfitting. However, to obtain a
valid comparison with the baseline model, the SBI method [23], we
used the same number of epochs (100) to train the SBI method.
Preprocessing. The preprocessing pipeline applies a range of aug-
mentations to enhance model robustness. For source images, trans-
formations like RGB shift, hue, saturation, brightness, and contrast
adjustments are applied, along with downscaling and sharpening. Addi-
tional augmentations, including image compression, are applied to both
source and target images. These transformations simulate real-world
variations and improve the model’s generalization.
Training. For the CNN model, we fine-tuned the EfficientNet-B5 model
that was pre-trained on ImageNet. We employed the Sharpness-Aware
Minimization (SAM) optimizer to optimize the training process. We set
the momentum for the optimizer to 0.9 and the learning rate to 0.001.
The learning rate linearly decays after 75 epochs. We train the model
on a batch size of 16. The size of the image is set to 380 × 380.
Inference. During the inference stage, a set consisting of 32 frames was
extracted from each video. If the frame contains two or more faces,

4.2. Results
In this section, we present the evaluation results of the proposed
FSBI approach for deepfake detection. Two settings were used to eval-
uate the proposed approach: within-dataset and cross-dataset. The Area
Under the Curve (AUC) metric was used in both settings.
Within-dataset Evaluation. In this evaluation, the performance of the
FSBI was evaluated using the same dataset on which it was trained.
Table 1 presents the results obtained using the FF++ and Celeb-DF
datasets. Deepfake images of the FF++ dataset are available in four
formats based on the deepfake generation technique (DF, FS, F2F,
and NT), as discussed in Section 4.1. We evaluated the performance
of FSBI on all versions of FF++. Our model reported high AUC for
both datasets, as indicated at the top of Table 1. For FF++, the FSBI
approach can recognize deepfakes generated using almost all manipu-
lation methods. A high AUC was obtained for the FF++ dataset when
deepfake images were generated using the DF method. In contrast, a
low AUC was obtained when the NT manipulation technique was used.
This could be attributed to the fact that the NT method introduces a
novel paradigm for image synthesis. NTs are learned feature maps that
store high-dimensional information on 3D mesh proxies, which differ
significantly from the traditional textures used in other deepfake gen-
eration techniques. This unique representation presents challenges for
deepfake detection models, reducing their effectiveness. In addition, the
compressed videos of the FF++ dataset (FF++(LQ)) had low resolution.
Detecting deepfakes in low-resolution images is more challenging than
that in raw high-resolution images. For the Celeb-DF dataset, a high
AUC value was obtained when the approach was trained and evaluated
using samples from the same dataset.
Cross-dataset Evaluation. A crucial aspect of assessing the generaliz-
ability of our approach is to evaluate its performance across different
data distributions and manipulation techniques. This type of evaluation
is called a cross-dataset evaluation and serves as a robust test to gauge
the effectiveness and adaptability of any deepfake detection approach.
In this evaluation, the dataset used to test the model differed from
that used for training. We evaluated the FSBI using the evaluation
settings, and the results are presented in Table 1. Notably, when the
model was trained on the FF++ dataset and tested on the Celeb-DF
dataset, it outperformed related methods, achieving an impressive AUC
of 95.49%. Furthermore, training the model on the Celeb-DF dataset
yielded satisfactory results when evaluated using the FF++ dataset. The
model was evaluated on the FF++ dataset using various manipulation
techniques. The table shows that FSBI achieved a high AUC for the
deepfakes generated using the DF and FS methods. However, the AUC
scores for the FSBI model using the F2F and NT methods were relatively
low. The results with NT manipulations aligned with those obtained in
the within-dataset evaluation.

DWT Variables. DWT plays a crucial role in the FSBI approach for
representing and extracting features from SBI images in the frequency
domain. Three main wavelet types are available for DWT: Biorthogonal,
Symlet, and Haar, and the choice among them depends on the down-
stream task. Additionally, three signal extension modes—Symmetric,
Reflective, and Antireflective—can be applied to reduce artifacts result-
ing from signal extrapolation during DWT computation. To evaluate
the impact of wavelet type and signal extension modes on FSBI per-
formance, we conducted a cross-dataset evaluation, and the results
are shown in Table 5. The results reveal that the choice of DWT
variables significantly influences the model’s performance, with the
best configuration being the Symlet wavelet and Reflective mode (M5
model), achieving an AUC of 95.49%. This underscores the importance
of carefully selecting DWT parameters to optimize the model’s ability to
detect deepfake images. Notably, all DWT configurations outperformed
the state-of-the-art results on the Celeb-DF dataset, supporting our
hypothesis about the effectiveness of DWT for deepfake detection.
Image Size Selection. The choice of image size is crucial for FSBI
performance. We experimented with four image sizes: 170×170, 230 ×
230, 380×380, and 512 × 512. As shown in Table 4, notable differences
in AUC scores were observed with varying image resolutions. Perfor-
mance improved with larger image sizes, as expected, due to more
visible artifacts. However, the model’s performance deteriorated with
the largest image size of 512 × 512 pixels, likely due to information
loss during interpolation from 256 × 256 to 512 × 512.
Choice of the pretrained Model.While the CNN model used the
pre-trained EfficientNet-B5 in the FSBI framework, we also evaluated
other network architectures. Our experiments included EfficientNet-
B4 [51], EfficientNet-B5, ResNet50 [66], and MobileNetV2 [67]. We
also present standard SBI results using these models. As shown in
Table 6, EfficientNet-based models consistently outperformed ResNet50
and MobileNetV2 across various configurations. EfficientNet-B5 im-
proved the performance of SBI and FSBI by more than 1% compared to
EfficientNet-B4, demonstrating that selecting the appropriate network
architecture is vital for enhancing deepfake detection accuracy.

4.4. Comparisons
This section compares our FSBI approach with SOTA deepfake
detection techniques. In this comparison, two evaluation settings were
used: within-dataset and cross-dataset, as shown in Table 2. For within-
dataset evaluation, FF++ was used to train and test the models. Most
deepfake detection techniques use this dataset for within-dataset eval-
uations. To the best of our knowledge, the Celeb-DF dataset has been
used in the literature for cross-dataset evaluation.
We also conducted a cross-dataset evaluation using Celeb-DF as
the testing dataset. Unlike approaches that use multiple datasets for
training [18,60,62,63], our approach uses only the FF++ dataset. To
ensure a fair comparison with the SBI approach [23], we reproduced
their results using the compressed c23 version (FF++(LQ)) of the FF++
dataset. As shown in Table 2, the proposed approach significantly
outperformed all other techniques. In addition, it outperformed the
approaches trained on multiple datasets.
To visualize the qualitative differences between the proposed FSBI
approach and the SBI method [23], t-SNE was applied to the feature
vectors from the last layer of both models. Fig. 4 illustrates the feature
space visualizations of the FSBI and SBI methods when trained on the
FF++ and Celeb-DF datasets. As shown in the feature visualization plot,
the FSBI method generated a distinct boundary between the clusters
of the two classes for the FF++ dataset. In contrast, the SBI method
exhibited less well-defined boundaries between the two classes. For the
Celeb-DF dataset, the FSBI method demonstrated significantly reduced
overlap between the two clusters compared to the SBI method. Fig. 3
shows some deepfake samples that were correctly detected by our
approach, whereas the SBI method failed to detect them. The figure
also includes other samples that were challenging for both methods.
5. Conclusion
In this work, we present a novel deepfake detection method, ESBI,
which significantly outperforms existing methodologies. Our approach
leverages the synergy of SBI and DWT, enabling our model to detect
subtle artifacts in manipulated content. Through extensive experiments
and ablation studies, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our pro-
posed method, achieving a remarkable AUC of 95.49%. The strategic
combination of FSBI with the EfficientNet-B5 architecture showcases
the potential for enhanced generalization and adaptability. Our results
establish a new benchmark in cross-dataset evaluation, underscoring
the efficacy of our approach in combating deepfake threats across
diverse datasets.
Despite these advancements, several directions for future research
remain. First, enhancing the robustness of FSBI against adversarially
generated deepfakes warrants exploration, as attackers continually
evolve techniques to bypass detection methods. Second, investigating
the integration of temporal features from video data could further
improve detection accuracy by capturing inconsistencies over time.
Additionally, extending the framework to handle emerging modalities,
such as audio–visual deepfakes or 3D manipulations, could broaden its
applicability.
