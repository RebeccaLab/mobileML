Abstract—Deepfakes made with advanced deep learning 
methods are a big threat to the safety, privacy, and integrity of 
digital media. It's getting harder to tell when audio and video 
content has been changed because they look more real. Recent 
research has proposed various deepfake detection methods, 
ranging from unimodal approaches to multimodal frameworks 
that jointly analyse audio and visual information.  However, 
many existing solutions are computationally expensive or lack 
interpretability, limiting their suitability for real-time and 
forensic applications. The state-of-the-art deepfake detection 
methods are thoroughly reviewed in this paper, with particular 
attention paid to model architectures, datasets, evaluation 
metrics, and significant limitations. Lightweight models, 
multimodal fusion techniques, and explainable artificial 
intelligence (XAI) methods are given special attention. A 
lightweight multimodal deepfake detection framework is 
proposed based on the identified research gaps. It uses Grad-
CAM for explainable decision making, late fusion for 
multimodal integration, and convolutional neural networks for 
audio and video feature extraction. In order to make it 
appropriate for real-time deployment, the suggested framework 
seeks to strike an efficient balance between detection 
performance, computational efficiency, and interpretability. 
In addition, a structured experimental validation framework a
nd computational efficiency analysis strategy are presented to f
acilitate practical implementation and benchmarking against e
xisting multimodal techniques. 
Keywords—Multimodal, CNN, GRAD-CAM, Deepfake, XAI, 
Late Fusion. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Deepfakes, which are artificially altered audio, video, or 
image content intended to look real, have become increasingly 
common as a result of the quick development of deep learning 
and generative models. Although there are valid uses for these 
technologies in virtual communication and entertainment, 
their abuse poses major risks to social stability, digital trust, 
privacy, and security. Reliable detection mechanisms are a 
critical research priority because deepfakes are increasingly 
being used for political manipulation, identity fraud, 
disinformation campaigns, and the creation of non-consensual 
content. 
Visual artifacts in manipulated videos, such as strange 
facial expressions, irregular eye blinking, and inconsistent 
blending, were the main focus of early deepfake detection 
techniques. When dealing with high-quality or compressed 
content, these video-based techniques frequently failed, 
despite achieving respectable performance in controlled 
environments. 
Simultaneously, 
audio-based 
deepfake 
detection methods were developed to detect synthetic speech 
produced by text-to-speech and voice cloning systems. Single-
modal methods are still susceptible, though, since attackers 
can control one modality while maintaining the relative 
authenticity of the other. 
Recent research has moved toward multimodal deepfake 
detection, which simultaneously examines audio and video 
data, in order to get around these restrictions. By taking 
advantage 
of 
cross-modal 
inconsistencies, 
such 
as 
mismatched lip movements and speech patterns, multimodal 
systems provide increased robustness. Despite their efficacy, 
a number of multimodal approaches currently in use rely on 
computationally costly architectures, such as transformers and 
large-scale attention models, which limit their use in real-time 
and resource-constrained settings. 
Although transformer-based multimodal systems have a 
high detection rate, their enormous parameter size and 
attention-based computations considerably increase memory 
and inference overhead. This limits their use in edge devices 
and real-time forensics systems. As a result, a computationally 
efficient but interpretable multimodal solution is necessary. 
The inability of existing deepfake detection systems to be 
interpreted is another significant drawback. The majority of 
deep learning models operate as "black boxes," producing 
classification results without providing an explanation for 
their choices. In high-stakes applications where explainability 
and trust are crucial, like digital forensics, law enforcement, 
and content moderation, this lack of transparency is especially 
problematic. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods 
that can identify the areas or characteristics affecting a model's 
prediction are therefore in greater demand. 
To address these issues, this paper provides a thorough 
analysis of deepfake detection techniques, including 
explainable methods, lightweight architectures, and single-
modal and multimodal approaches. A lightweight and 
comprehensible multimodal deepfake detection framework is 
also suggested based on the survey's findings. The framework 
uses a fully connected layer for classification, late fusion to 
combine multimodal representations, and Convolutional 
Neural Networks (CNNs) for effective audio and video feature 
extraction. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping 
(Grad-CAM) is used to provide visual explanations for both 
audio and video predictions in order to improve transparency.

II. LITERATURE REVIEW 
A. Video-Based Deepfake Detection 
Video-based deepfake detection methods analyse spatial 
and temporal inconsistencies introduced during face 
manipulation. Early studies relied on handcrafted features 
such as eye blinking frequency, facial landmarks, and head 
pose estimation to detect unnatural patterns. These approaches 
provided good interpretability but lacked robustness against 
high-quality deepfakes.  
With the adoption of deep learning, CNN-based 
architectures 
became 
widely 
used 
for 
extracting 
discriminative visual features from facial regions. Shallow 
CNNs such as MesoNet demonstrated that lightweight 
architectures could achieve reasonable accuracy with low 
computational overhead. However, deeper CNNs and 
temporal models, including 3D CNNs and CNN–RNN 
hybrids, were later introduced to capture motion-based 
inconsistencies. Although these methods improved detection 
accuracy, they significantly increased model complexity and 
inference time. 
B. Audio-Based Deepfake Detection 
Audio deepfake detection focuses on identifying synthetic 
speech generated by voice conversion and text-to-speech 
systems. Traditional signal-processing approaches analysed 
spectral features and prosodic cues, but these methods were 
insufficient for detecting modern, high-quality synthetic 
audio.  
Recent works employ CNNs trained on Mel-spectrograms 
or MFCC representations to capture time–frequency artifacts 
unique to synthetic speech. CNN-based audio detectors have 
shown strong performance and relatively low computational 
cost. Nevertheless, audio-only detection remains vulnerable 
when the visual modality is manipulated independently or 
when high-fidelity voice cloning is used. 
C. Multimodal Deepfake Detection 
Multimodal deepfake detection aims to improve 
robustness by jointly analysing audio and video modalities. 
These approaches exploit cross-modal inconsistencies such as 
lip–speech mismatch and audiovisual synchronization errors. 
Fusion strategies are typically categorized as early fusion, late 
fusion, or hybrid fusion.  
While transformer-based multimodal frameworks with 
cross-modal attention have demonstrated high detection 
accuracy, they require substantial computational resources 
and large training datasets. In contrast, late fusion methods 
that combine modality-specific features at the decision level 
offer a more efficient alternative, though they remain 
underexplored in lightweight and explainable settings. 
D.  Lightweight and Explainable Approaches 
The need for real-time deployment has motivated research 
into lightweight deepfake detection models. Compact CNN 
architectures 
and 
mobile-optimized 
networks 
reduce 
parameter count and inference latency while maintaining 
acceptable performance. However, many lightweight models 
compromise interpretability and provide limited insights into 
decision-making.  
Explainable AI techniques, particularly Grad-CAM, have 
been applied to CNN-based deepfake detectors to visualize 
regions influencing classification. In video-based detection, 
Grad-CAM highlights manipulated facial regions, while in 
audio-based detection, it identifies discriminative time–
frequency components in spectrograms. Despite their 
effectiveness, explainability is rarely integrated into 
multimodal deepfake detection frameworks. 
E. Recent Advances (2024-2026) 
Recent research has concentrated on transformer-based 
multimodal 
architectures 
and 
cross-modal 
attention 
techniques that improve semantic consistency between audio 
and visual streams. While these models improve detection 
robustness and cross-dataset generalization, they also add 
significant computational complexity. In parallel, lightweight 
CNN-based models for real-time and edge deployment 
situations have been developed, with an emphasis on low 
parameter count and efficient inference. These findings 
highlight the trade-off between detection performance and 
computing efficiency, emphasizing the importance of 
organized lightweight multimodal frameworks.

G. Research Gap 
TABLE I summarizes contemporary multimodal deepfake 
detection models that improve resilience via semantic 
consistency 
modeling 
and 
transformer-based 
fusion 
procedures. However, these structures add greatly to 
computational 
complexity 
and 
training 
requirements. 
Lightweight CNN-based techniques minimize model size, but 
they frequently lack structured multimodal fusion and 
embedded 
interpretability. 
Furthermore, 
explainability 
methodologies are usually used as post-hoc analysis rather 
than being incorporated into architectural design. These 
findings highlight the need for a lightweight, computationally 
efficient multimodal deepfake detection framework with 
structured explainability suitable for real-world use. 
III. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY AND ARCHITECTURE 
This section describes the proposed lightweight and 
interpretable deepfake detection framework, designed to 
operate under flexible input conditions. The framework 
supports audio-only, video-only, and combined audio–
video inputs, enabling robust detection across diverse real-
world scenarios. The proposed methodology emphasizes 
efficient feature extraction, multimodal learning, and 
explainability, making it suitable for real-time and resource-
constrained environments. 
A. System Overview 
The proposed framework employs modality-specific 
feature extraction pipelines that can operate independently or 
jointly depending on the availability of input modalities. 
When both audio and video streams are present, a late fusion 
strategy is applied to combine high-level representations. In 
unimodal scenarios, the framework performs classification 
using the available modality alone. 
Overall Processing Pipeline: 
1. The input sample may consist of audio-only, video-
only, or combined audio–video data. 
2. If video data is available, frames are sampled and 
facial regions are detected and aligned using 
MTCNN. 
3. If audio data is available, the audio signal is 
transformed into time–frequency representations. 
4. CNN-based models extract features independently 
from the available modalities. 
5. When both modalities are present, a late fusion 
mechanism combines multimodal features. 
6. A 
fully 
connected 
layer 
performs 
real/fake 
classification. 
7. Grad-CAM generates explainable visualizations for 
the model’s predictions. 
The overall architecture of the proposed framework is 
illustrated in FIG. 1, showing parallel audio and video 
pipelines with multimodal fusion, followed by explainable 
classification. 
B. Video Feature Extraction Module 
For video-based inputs, frames are uniformly sampled and 
processed through a facial preprocessing stage. Facial regions 
are detected and aligned using Multi-task Cascaded 
Convolutional Networks (MTCNN) to reduce background 
interference and enhance feature quality. The aligned facial 
frames are then passed to a lightweight CNN for spatial 
feature extraction. 
C. Audio Feature Extraction Module 
For audio-based inputs, the raw audio signal is converted 
into Mel-spectrogram representations and processed using a 
CNN-based audio feature extractor. This module captures 
spectral anomalies associated with synthetic or manipulated 
speech. 
D. Multimodal Late Fusion Strategy 
When both audio and video modalities are available, the 
proposed framework applies a late fusion strategy by 
concatenating the extracted audio and video feature vectors. 
This unified representation enables complementary learning 
across modalities and improves robustness against modality-
specific manipulation.

In unimodal scenarios, the fusion stage is bypassed, and 
classification is performed using the available modality alone. 
E. Classification Module 
The feature representation - either unimodal or multimodal 
- is passed through a lightweight fully connected layer 
followed by a SoftMax activation function to perform binary 
classification between real and deepfake samples. 
F. Explainability Using Grad-CAM 
Grad-CAM is applied to the convolutional layers of the 
active modality-specific CNNs. For video inputs, Grad-CAM 
highlights facial regions influencing the decision, while for 
audio inputs, it visualizes discriminative time–frequency 
regions in the spectrogram. In multimodal cases, explanations 
are generated independently for both modalities.

x

x

x

x

IV. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION PLAN 
Implementation and experimental evaluation of the 
suggested framework are presently underway to confirm its 
efficacy. To guarantee systematic benchmarking, the 
evaluation process listed below has been established. 
A. Datasets 
The system will be tested using publicly accessible 
benchmark datasets using a variety of processing methods and 
compression scenarios, including FaceForensics++, Celeb-
DF, and DFDC. 
B. Metrics for Evaluation 
Standard measures such as Accuracy, Precision, Recall, 
F1-score, and AUC-ROC will be used to evaluate 
performance. Class-wise detection performance will also be 
assessed using confusion matrix analysis. 
C. Analysis of Computational Efficiency 
The following metrics will be assessed in order to support 
the lightweight design: 
x 
Total number of parameters 
x 
Size of the model (MB) 
x 
Time spent inference per frame 
x 
GPU memory use 
D. Analysis via Comparison 
The suggested framework will be contrasted with: 
x 
Unimodal CNN-based models 
x 
Transformer-driven multimodal systems 
x 
Current models for explainable detection 
E. Analysis of Robustness 
Robustness will be assessed in light of: 
x 
Compression of videos 
x 
Noise that is additive 
x 
Testing across datasets 
V. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 
This paper provided a structured examination of current 
deepfake detection approaches, emphasizing the growing 
necessity 
for 
multimodal 
analysis 
and 
integrated 
interpretability. According to the review, unimodal systems 
frequently encounter robustness and generalization issues 
when confronted with advanced manipulation techniques, 
whereas recent multimodal and transformer-based models, 
while effective, add significant computational complexity. 
To 
overcome 
these 
issues, 
a 
lightweight 
and 
understandable multimodal deepfake detection system was 
presented. The architecture includes separate CNN-based 
audio and video processing pipelines, MTCNN-based face 
area extraction for accurate visual feature representation, and 
a late fusion technique for flexible modality merging. Grad-
CAM allows for structured visual explanations of model 
predictions, which improves transparency and user trust. 
At the time of submission, the suggested framework was 
still being implemented and experimentally validated. A 
complete evaluation strategy has been developed to evaluate
