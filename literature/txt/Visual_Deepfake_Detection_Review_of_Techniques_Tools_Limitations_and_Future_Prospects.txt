ABSTRACT In recent years, rapid advancements in deepfakes (incorporating Artificial Intelligence (AI),
machine, and deep learning) have updated tools and techniques for manipulating multimedia. Though
technology has primarily been utilized for beneficial purposes, such as education and entertainment, it is
also used for malicious or unethical tasks to spread disinformation or ruin someone’s dignity, even if it
encompasses harassing and blackmailing victims. Deepfakes refer to high-quality and realistic multimedia-
manipulated content that has been digitally modified or synthetically generated. We conducted a systematic
literature review of deepfake detection to offer an updated overview of existing research work that
initially describes the widely accessible deepfake generation tools, classifications, and detection process.
We highlighted recent techniques in visual deepfake detection based on the feature representations, grouped
into four domains: spatial, temporal, frequency, and spatio-temporal, including their key features and
limitations by providing details of existing datasets, together with the potentials of deepfake and its future
directions. This study tried to add an updated repository of technological change in deepfake, which could
help researchers to develop robust deepfake models.
INDEX TERMS Deepfake detection, machine learning, deep learning, deepfake applications, deepfake
datasets, deepfake generation tools.

is subsequently sent to the discriminator for analysis. The
more errors discovered in fake videos, the more likely it
is that the generator will be enhanced further for the next

B. DEEPFAKE CLASSIFICATION
In this article, deepfake is classified into four main categories
based on the facial manipulations they enabled: 1) Synthetic
face, 2) Facial reenactment, 3) Facial attribute alterations, and
4) Face swap.
1) SYNTHETIC FACE
Synthetic face is the generation of an artificial face, that does
not exist in real world. Synthetic face depends on discovering
occluded illustrations of the facial datasets in order to produce
a hyper-realist artificial face. The image’s output is not actual
since it was created without target features. Although the
modeling and gaming industries benefit from this technology,
an attacker may use it to steal someone else’s identity.
Yu et al. [48] introduced the Deep Convolutional Generative
Adversarial Network (DCGAN), a more stable computa-
tional architecture, to increase training stability and also
utilized deep convolutional networks instead of pooling and
batch normalizing, and based on an arithmetic vector, they
demonstrated an improved picture synthesis performance.
A new network architecture called Progressively Growing
Generative Adversarial Network (ProGAN) was released
by National Vision Instrument and Advanced Graphics
(NVIDIA) researchers a year later to improve end results’
reliability and accuracy within the learning procedure. As an
algorithm is trained, its characteristics gradually improve
from poor-quality data.
StyleGAN is a kind of network derived from ProGAN
[49]. After reviewing articles [50], [51], [52], it was observed
that the researchers changed the generator methodology
by utilizing Adaptive Instance Normalization (AdaIN) to
handle the creator training at each CNN layer. In accordance
with the provided vector, the creator usually synthesizes
uniform posture or style. Additionally, researchers intro-
duced random variation to the positioning of the synthetic
person’s pores, marks, beard, and hair. However, scientists
discovered that there were noticeable water droplet-like
anomalies in the artificial image as a result of StyleGAN’s
instance normalization. It is easily detectable due to its
faint trace. In their parallel studies in the same years, they
revised a new normalization strategy and released it as
StyleGANv2 [53]. They were able to smoothly remove the
artifacts and enhance the clarity of the image. Synthetic
face databases are frequently created using ProGAN and
StyleGAN [54].
2) FACE REENACTMENT
Reenactment, as opposed to face swap, is the transference of
an individual’s gaze or facial expression from source person
to target while maintaining the identity of target source. This
enabled the target to seem as though they are showing the
expression or addressing things that they haven’t said never.
This method was widely used prior to the development of
deepfake. The old methods obtain similar outcomes by using
computer graphics. Blanz et al., for instance, used a 3D
morphable model that was taken through the database of 3D
scans to reproduce the input image [55]. Thies et al. allowed
real time facial expression transmission by recording face
performance using a common Red Green Blue (RGB)-depth
sensor. They employed a mouth retrieval synthesis and a
parametric model to alter the target’s features to match the
source expression in order to produce a high quality outcome
[41], [56].
3) FACIAL ATTRIBUTE ALTERATIONS
Facial attribute alterations involve transforming some fea-
tures of the face, including skin tone, eye color, age,
hairstyles, and expression by preserving the target identity.
It has the ability to change someone’s look based on prede-
termined parameters. Star Generative Adversarial Network
(StarGAN) was the exemplary domain to domain translation
network for this technique [57]. In contrast to previously
published studies [58], [59], [60], [61], by employing a mask
vector approach, StarGAN facilitated multi domain training
and emphasized style transformation across domains. Xiao
et al. introduced elegant, a comparable multi attribute Cycle
Generative Adversarial Network (CycleGAN) based trans-
lation network [62]. Researchers concentrated on learning
models utilizing reconstruction loss, adversarial loss, and
domain classification loss. Although the style transfer results
were impressive, they are less acceptable than StarGAN since
the modified outputs can contain unwanted artifacts.
4) FACE SWAP
Face swap entails changing faces from source to target
while maintaining the facial expression of target person.
A quick face swap technique was presented by Korshunova
et al. in 2017 [63] to switch the identification of person
A to B as long as the lighting setup, head position, and
facial expression remain the same. Unlike a style transfer,
where the content takes up the remaining space, this one
describes the individual’s style. The researchers employed
modified MTCNNs with loss of content, style, and light
function to transform the contents of A to B. They used
68 facial landmarks to align the resulting face using an affine
transformation, and a segmentation mask was used to stitch
the backdrop. Once trained to comprehend A’s data, the
neural network can have several targets, B, which enables a
one to many face swapping method.

C. DEEPFAKE DETECTION PROCESS
The primary concerns of deepfake detection are facial
expressions, face alignment, and face classification. Facial
features are the most fundamental step in deepfake detection.
The performance of deepfake defections depends upon the
excellent degree of official expression [64]. Identification of
the face is a primary self regulating job in different appli-
cations, such as identity revivification systems, intelligent
inspection systems, and automated immigration systems.
Deepfake detection could be distributed into two branches:
face identification and face verification. Face identification
detects the most similar image from the given images.
In contrast, face verification means that the given pair of
images belong to the same or different pair of images [64],
as shown in Figure 3.
Over the years, facial feature detection has become
increasingly popular and stronger in research and practical
applications. A well-known success in deep learning has
encouraged facial feature reorganization. Conversely, facial
wrapping relies on the expression, movement, and look of the
face as well as the facade. In these cases, identifying a realistic
face is a complex process. Gathering and classifying a large
amount of data is a difficult and time-consuming operation.
The data is also highly expensive and has fault tolerance;
the publicly available datasets could not bear any variations.
Face data augmentation comes into the cross to overcome the
deficiency of facial training datasets. Face data augmentation
is a technology that modifies or imitates virtual face patterns,
as shown in Figure 4 to change gathered factual face patterns,
increasing the data quantity.
Face data augmentation overcomes existing data limita-
tions by enlarging or transforming the actual or simulated
virtual face sample. Inadequate and limited data leads
to accuracy and overwhelming inefficiency because of
over-parameterization and overfitting problems. Face data
augmentation plays a vital role in the performance of neural
networks, such as 1) Desired features and attributes of faces
could be obtained by applying controlling factors. (2) It is
very cheap in this way that, with a little bit of transformation,
it can get enlarged data as compared to the pileup and classify
the actual data. (3) Artificially manufactured data has no
bugs as compared to real-world data. (4) Data Augmentation
has remarkable benefits like self-obstruction and equality
of datasets with more intra-class variations [11]. Image
augmentation could be achieved by flipping and rotating,
cropping and resizing, color jittering, image warping, random
erasing, and by adding noise patterns like as Gaussian,
Rayleigh, Erlang (or gamma), exponential, uniform, impulse,
salt and paper, speckle, poison, quantization, shot, and
temporal noise.

a: PHOTO RESPONSE NON UNIFORMITY (PRNU) BASED

Every digital image sensor introduces a signal that simu-

from differences in sensor pixels during the photoelectric
conversion process. As a result, the PRNU can be seen as
an inherent characteristic of each captured digital image.
Koopman et al. [91] suggested the only Photo Response Non
Uniformity (PRNU) based deepfake detection method. They
demonstrated a considerable variance in the average scaled
cross-correlation score between real and deepfake videos by
comparing PRNU patterns of several frames within the same
video. Their research, however, was restricted to a (private)
self-made dataset with 16 deepfakes and 10 real videos.
Lugstein et al. [92] reported that PRNU based deepfake
detection is a modification to earlier methods by Scherhag
et al. [93] and Rathgeb et al. [94], which were utilized for
the identification of different types of image manipulations,
such as face morphing assaults on passport photos and facial
retouching via beautifying apps. To detect deepfakes, they
employed the same concept and utilized the suggested PRNU
characteristics.

a pre-trained CNN model, filtering out irrelevant content
and retaining essential discriminative pixels for detection.
The transformation model is fixed, allowing the gradients to
serve as a generalized artifact representation, which reduces
reliance on training data and enhances generalization but
has the limitation that the proposed framework primarily
focuses on detecting GAN-generated images. However, it has
limitations when it comes to detecting non-GAN models for
deepfake detection.
It is frequently impossible to obtain the pre-trained

get better results from the trained datasets. By applying
a series of geometric and photometric transformations on
training datasets also resolves the overfitting problems.
However, different researchers utilized different techniques
to overcome the problem of overfitting and made handsome
approaches to achieve the generalization. Prajapati and Pollett

In order to detect GAN generated images, Li et al. [100]

2) VISUAL ARTIFACTS BASED DETECTION
Due to processing power and generation time constraints,
deepfake algorithms can only create face images of a specific
size. They must go through affine warping to fit the source’s
facial arrangement. Face warping causes various artifacts as
a result of the difference in resolution across the surrounding
features and the distorted facial region. Realistic images are
trained with the variational auto-encoder; this classifies them
and other images treated as fake images, as proposed by
Khalid et al. [105].
In order to identify face swapping techniques, Li et al.
[106] identified the blending boundaries; a second approach
involves using neural networks, such as customized deep
networks [107], traditional neural networks and so on,
to capture the forged features. The efficiency of the neural
approaches was incredibly great [108]. However, the reliance
on training datasets substantially restricts the ability to
generalize the model, which is crucial for practical use.
Hong et al. [109] suggested a technique in which every
frame in a real video should have a consistent fingerprint or
artifacts. On the other hand, deepfakes inherently generates
inconsistent artifacts due to the substituted face areas. In order
to identify the discrepancy from the generated data, they
suggested a self-supervised deepfake identification technique
that uses an Artifact Discrepant Data Generator (ADDG)
and a Deepfake Artifact Discrepancy Detector (DADD). The
ADDG generates fake samples by altering the facial area
using conventional processing tools, utilizing only real video
frames. The DADD uses a multi-task learning method, links
each sub-task to a particular kind of generated data, and
integrates the sub-tasks to achieve the final results. These
methods are beneficial as the visual artifacts are adequately
defined.
a: FACIAL LANDMARK BASED DETECTION
In deepfake detection, facial landmark analysis analyses
the stability and coherence of facial features (eyes, nose,
eyebrows, chin, mouth, chin and jawline) to determine
differences between synthetic and real data. The method
utilizes distinct spatial relationships, landmark consistency,
and feature symmetry to improve detection accuracy. In the
dlib library, 68 (x, y)-coordinates corresponding to facial
features on the face are estimated utilizing a trained facial
landmark detector [63].
Yang et al. [110] presented a new technique to identify
deepfakes by examining variations in head positions that
are inferred from facial landmarks. It draws attention to the
fact that deepfakes frequently provide misaligned landmark
positions, which causes notable variations in predicted head
poses. The study acknowledges that head posture analysis
can be used to identify landmark location inconsistencies,
even if they might not be visually noticeable. In situations
where landmark detection is inadequate, landmark accuracy
could reduce the model’s efficacy. The author Li et al.
[111] study is based on a novel image representation known
as face X-ray. As a typical face transformation technique,
this representation helped determine whether an input face
image is a blend of two distinct images. The framework
depends on the blending phase rather than particular artifacts
from manipulation techniques, improving its applicability
in various situations. When working with low-resolution
images, performance decreases because the fake evidence is
less noticeable. The current arms race between fake creation
and detection is a typical problem in forgery detection. Liao
et al. [112] presented a novel work based on the Facial
Muscle Motion (FAMM) for detecting deepfake social short
videos such as TikTok or Facebook reels. They focused
on continual facial landmarks taken from video frames; the
FAMM framework creates facial muscle motion features.
The method aids in spotting strange facial expressions that
are a sign of deepfakes. In this approach, a new method
of video forensics was adopted called the Dempster-Shafer
theory, which combines various forensic expertise to improve
detection accuracy. The authors claimed that the model have
good performance on FaceForensics++ (FF++) dataset but
not for others. The study admits that the various coding
techniques social networks employ to compress videos could
provide new difficulties that the studies did not fully explore.

B. FREQUENCY DOMAIN BASED DETECTION
Frequency is the rate of change in an image. High frequencies
are seen in areas of the image where the color changes rapidly,
like sharp edges, while low frequencies are found in areas
where the color changes gradually, such as broad surfaces
with solid color. The real images have a higher frequency
distribution than the fake images, and these variations are
helpful in the detection of deepfakes as shown in Table 5.
Durall et al. [142] are the first research group to focus
on the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) to detect face
manipulation. They found that the deepfake information has
been detected by utilizing the average amplitude of each
frequency band Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) is used by
F3-Net [143] to retrieve frequency domain data and examine
the computational aspects of face forgery detection. The color
combination in GAN images and the type of camera used
this information to design the network, utilizing the prior
knowledge proposed by McCloskey and Albright [144].

spectrum discussed by Frank et al. [145]. While presenting a
modification of the localization architecture utilizing spatial
maps and frequency domain association, Jeong et al. [146]

fingerprints in the frequency domain. GAN generated images
have different frequency domain distributions from real
images. By adopting the distribution of frequency domains,
it could predict the deepfake because the primary charac-
teristic of Frequency-level Perturbation GAN (FrePGAN) is
ability to produce frequency level perturbation maps, which
aid in blurring the boundary between faked and original
photos. Enhancing the generalization of deepfake detectors
across different GAN models is based upon this. The
frequency perturbation of FrePGAN and pixels distribution
are the two primary parts of the model. Together, these
modules improve detection capabilities by concentrating on
irregularities across the pixel and frequency levels.
The tendency of detectors to overfit to training conditions
because of the distinctive appearances of frequency level
artifacts which can differ depending on CNN architectures
and training categories is one of the major drawbacks
that has been found. Wang et al. [147] proposed that
deepfake detection can extract richer attributes according
to the suggested strategy, which combines information

C. TEMPORAL DOMAIN BASED DETECTION
Deepfakes are also generated based on temporal information.
Temporal information could be defined as videos comprising
a series of visual frames, while these frames include incon-
sistencies or chronological data. Frames of a video change
concerning time; this information is known as temporal
information. Inconsistencies and chronological data provide
an excellent source for detecting deepfakes, as discussed in
Table 6.
1) TEMPORAL INCONSISTENCIES BASED DETECTION
Flickering between adjacent styled frames and uneven
stylization of moving objects are two visual cues that
indicate temporal inconsistencies [162]. For deepfake detec-
tion videos, temporal inconsistencies between the video
frames provide vital cues. Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)s
or other similar networks are used to determine these
inconsistencies.
Gu et al. [163] provide a novel framework for deepfake
video detection called region-aware temporal inconsistency
learning to enable various regions to acquire self-unique
temporal inconsistency. This system automatically creates
relevant temporal filters. The Cross-Snippet (CS) attention
module enables the CS information to deal with deeper
representations when coupled with the specifically created
sampling technique. A temporal aware model was proposed
Guera et al. [164] to reorganize deepfake videos; a CNN
was used for feature-level extractions from frames. After
this, these extracted feature frames were sent to the LSTM
layer [165] to check the manipulation sequences between the
frames. At last, videos were organized as fake or real with
the help of the softmax function. We can determine the per-
pixel motion behavior called optical mechanism flow from
the contagious frames. Gu et al. [166] presented a system that
combines the temporal feature analysis skills of LSTM with
the feature extraction capabilities of CNN. This framework is
based on the extraction of temporal inconsistencies. Hosier
et al. [167] proposed that video temporal points could be
detected from the speed manipulation, and re-encoded frames
predict the deleted or newly added frames between the videos.

between authentic and fraudulent videos by comparing
multimedia and emotion-based variations in speech patterns
and face movements. In addition, Chung et al. suggested
[170] utilizing the 3DCNN for feature extraction in space
and time domains. The research discussed in [171] combined
visual and audio characteristics as inputs for LSTM networks
for examining speech consistency, but it was ineffective
in detecting deepfakes [21]. Deepfake is detected using
visual artifacts such as mouth area and audio associated
with lip-syn. Agarwal et al. [172] notify in their proposed
scheme that deepfake video phonemes (spoken words) do
not match with the visemes (outer region of mouth). This
method was only related to the Barack Obama deepfake
videos. While pronouncing these words M, B, and P, the

Boundaries, a multimodal method called temporal forgery

By examining the background components of a manipulated
video or image, deepfake detection utilized background
consistency checks for irregularities that might point to
tampering. Although deepfake techniques are improving
at creating realistic backgrounds and facial features, they
frequently have trouble consistently handling background
aspects. Background analysis is a helpful technique for
detecting deepfakes because of this flaw, which can show
up as blurring, unnatural warping, inconsistent illumination,
or uneven depth. The background consistencies are based

In order to enhance face forgery detection, the study

to suppress certain forging trails, enabling a more reliable
detection process. It uses a fine-grained relation learning
prototype that employs instance and local similarity-aware
losses to mine essential information in forgeries. The
research article [175] noted that although compression can
remove important forging information, it is still challenging
to derive discriminative features from heavily compressed

b: HEART RATE VARIABILITY (HRV) BASED DETECTION
Heart Rate Variability (HRV) based deepfake detection uses
the microscopic changes in skin tone and face texture
to assess physiological signals like pulse rates that are
challenging for deepfake algorithms to replicate accurately.
Even a slight blood flow variation that correlates with
heartbeats is the source of the mild color changes observed on
human faces in the original video. In contrast, deepfakes lack
a natural pulse-based pattern and usually focus on realistic
facial characteristics rather than physiological information.
The approach based on the remote photoplethysmography
(rPPG) to detect fake videos is proposed by Boccignone
et al. [197], exploring the complex structure of heart rate
dynamics retrieved from face video streams. The approach
depends on the agent-in-context (AIC) theory, arguing that
physiological signals are not manipulated in fake videos
compared to real nature. The approach estimated the Blood
Volume Pulse (BVP) signals from face patches and predicted
artificial manipulation using two sets of characteristics.
The complexity is measured by intra patch BVP and
designed to capture physiological behavior. Relying only on
physiological signals leads to a loss of transparency, which is
not a practical approach for deepfake detection. Moreover,
it is still liable after additional interferences, such as head
movements or changes in lighting. The research study [198]
employed a Beats Per Minute (BPM)s analysis approach
based on rPPG for deepfake detection. BPMs are computed
from videos to quantify the fluctuations in blood volume
based on skin color. The variations in BPMs between real
and fake videos revealed deepfake. The study highlighted that
deepfake detection models based on rPPG still have detection
accuracy and generalization performance limitations.

Deepfake detection algorithms effectively evaluate visual

of faked videos. The Spatial Reduction Attention Block

performance against deepfake datasets. The proposed net-

datasets: DFDC, FF++, Celeb-DF, and World Leaders. The
efficacy of networks against developing synthetic media
manipulation techniques may be impacted by the dependence

strategies. The article [228] suggested an Xception model that

implementation or assessment.

VII. CONCLUSION
Throughout this survey paper, we addressed a detailed
systematic literature review on deepfake technology for
insight view of the technological transformation in deepfake.
In this regard, we focused on a comprehensive overview of
the deepfake generation process using GANs and encoder-
decoder pairs. The classification of deepfake was based on
four categories: synthetic face, facial reenactment, facial
attribute alterations, and face swap. Current tools for deep-
fake generation and beneficial and malicious applications
of deepfake were also discussed in this review. A detailed
empirical study was also performed concerning the deepfake
detection classifiers, their key features, and their limitations.
The currently available datasets for deepfake detection, most
commonly used in deepfake technology, were also described
in detail. This article will help owners and developers
of deepfake detection techniques to enhance their models
by upgrading knowledge and incorporating certain datasets
known to yield better results for the particular model type they
work with. Well-defined approaches are utilized; however,
in the deepfake detection process, heavy augmentation affects
the detection accuracy and requires heavy-duty consumption
resources for computations, so much attention is needed to
generate robust techniques considering data augmentation for
deepfake detection. As the deepfake generation process is
relatively easy and is generated through mobile apps now
a days, there must be attention to developing mobile apps
that will be proficient for end users to detect deepfake.
Despite substantial improvements, there are a lot of issues
with the present detection techniques that must be resolved
to detect deepfakes properly. Real-time deepfake detection
and utilization of detection techniques in many industries and
on various channels would be a significant challenge because
of its complications, including the computational capacity
required for deepfake detection in real time, given the
tremendous amount of information sent on the internet every
second. The requirement is that these detection techniques
work well with very few false positives. In order to achieve
this goal, one can use sophisticated learning techniques
such as meta-learning and metrics, as well as efficient
designs, such as compression techniques and transformers,
for example, quantitation, and determined investments in
a solid foundation of hardware and software. Furthermore,
the selection or rejection criteria process may have led
to some errors in sorting these studies. In this article,
we have gathered the relevant papers from several digital
repositories, publications, workshops, and conferences. It is
still conceivable that our compilation of research studies does
not include all relevant publications.
