A B S T R A C T
Deepfake detection has become a critical component of digital forensics and security, as manipulated images and 
videos increasingly threaten trust in visual media. However, existing methods often struggle with robustness 
under post-processing operations such as JPEG compression, Gaussian blur, scaling, and filtering, and with the 
growing diversity and realism of face image modification (FIM) forgeries. This work proposes CMNV2, a hybrid 
architecture that integrates MobileNetV2 with a custom CAFFE block to enhance feature extraction and classi­
fication accuracy. By adding five additional layers to a pre-trained structure, the model demonstrates superior 
resilience against complex real-world conditions and achieves 99.10% accuracy across multiple datasets, out­
performing 13 baseline CNN models. The study trained and tested CMNV2 on 5,000 images (real and deepfake 
faces), using a combination of deep neural networks (DNNs), transfer learning (TL), and deep learning (DL) 
techniques. Compared to 13 CNN-based architectures, the proposed model achieved superior performance across 
some important evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, error rate, and computational 
efficiency. These results highlight hybrid CMNV2 as a robust and efficient solution for deepfake face detection 
and classification, with potential applications in security, healthcare, and education.

Liveness-insensitive facial recognition technology can be fooled by 
modified copies of real people. Face credibility is a problem for

current methods cannot always verify a face’s authenticity. Advance­
ments in image-capturing and display technologies enable more realistic

The scope of the article is focused on deepfake face classification 
using both image and video data through a deep learning-based hybrid 
model named hybridCMNV2. The study involves: 
i. Improving MobileNetV2 through additional layers.
ii. Integrating a CAFFE-based module for faster feature embedding. iii. 
Applying the model specifically for binary classification of facial

Experiment setup
“Ciplab@Yonsei University” developed the original images dataset, 
which is accessible in the Kaggle repository [40]. This study divides 2, 
040 human face images into 2 categories. This study covers training and 
testing of 2 binary classification models 3.1. In this research, 2,040 
images are augmented to 5,000 images of 2 classes by flipping 180 de­
grees horizontally and cropping to increase model training chances.
The dataset used in this study comprises 5,000 facial images, equally 
divided into 2,500 real and 2,500 deepfake samples. Real images were 
sourced from publicly available facial datasets such as CelebA and LFW, 
while deepfake images were obtained from DeepFakeDetection (DFD) 
and FaceForensics++, ensuring a wide range of manipulation tech­
niques and content diversity. All images were carefully pre-processed 
with resizing to (224 × 224), grayscale-to-RGB conversion where 
needed, and normalization. Additionally, we applied data augmentation 
techniques including rotation, horizontal flipping, brightness adjust­
ment, and zoom to increase diversity and prevent overfitting. The 
dataset was also manually reviewed to validate labels and maintain class 
balance, ensuring the fairness and robustness of the binary classification 
process.
The image selection approach: initial random sampling followed by 
automated curation. Quality control measures, including:
Minimum image resolution requirement.
Face alignment using facial landmark detection.
Verification of full face visibility and absence of occlusion.
Manual inspection of a subset to confirm automated filtering.
Removal of duplicate or corrupted images.
Images from each dataset were initially randomly sampled to avoid 
selection bias. An automated curation process using a facial landmark 
detection algorithm ensured proper alignment. Images not meeting a 
minimum resolution of (224 × 224) pixels, those with partially occluded 
faces, or exhibiting significant compression artifacts were excluded. A 
subset of images was manually inspected to verify quality. Duplicates 
and corrupted files were removed, resulting in a clean dataset of 5,000 
images for training and testing the CMNV2 model.

Proposed methodology
Building on prior CMNV2 variants for face mask detection and face 
classification [21–23], we introduce a modified CMNV2 with five 
additional layers tailored for binary deepfake detection in photo and 
real-time video settings. Unlike earlier CMNV2 studies that targeted 
masks or multi-class real/fake categorization, our focus is forensic 
robustness to post-processing (JPEG compression, Gaussian blur, 
scaling, filtering) and comparative evaluation against 13 CNN baselines. 
It should be noted that a DNN structure developed using Keras, Ten­
sorFlow, and OpenCV modules accurately classifies and detects real and 
deepfake faces in real-time video and photos by our technique to classify 
and detect images. The improvised MobileNetV2 framework with the 
different layers added is shown in Fig. 2.
The CAFFE module in the proposed CMNV2 model refers to a 
custom-designed convolutional block inspired by efficient deep learning 
principles aimed at Fast Feature Embedding (FFE). This module consists 
of a sequence of depthwise separable convolutions, batch normalization, 
and ReLU activations, which accelerate computation while preserving 
discriminative power. The goal is to quickly reduce spatial dimensions 
and extract relevant patterns that are crucial for distinguishing between

MobileNetV2 for quicker classification and detection. CAFFE is a deep 
learning framework that trains and uses deep neural networks fast. Its 
speed and adaptability make it perfect for many computer vision ap­
plications. The foundation of standard MobileNetV2 is the integration of 
ImageNet [43] and Keras [44] applications into a pre-trained architec­
ture. In-depth descriptions of the layers are provided below:

and have red, green and blue (RGB) colour channels.
ii. Freezing MobileNetV2 Layers: The Standard MobileNetV2 
framework depends mainly on feature extraction. MobileNetV2 is 
a DCNN for image categorization. Importantly, the layer weights 
are “frozen” to maintain consistency. iii. Transfer learning: It uses 
a dataset to adapt a model trained for one task to another. This

Transfer learning refers to the technique of applying a model trained 
on one task to a related task that has a smaller dataset. The proposed 
hybrid CMNV2 model was developed using this approach. Baseline 
CNN, models are built from the ground up and trained on large volumes 
of labeled data for specific tasks. In contrast, transfer learning leverages 
pre-trained machine learning and deep learning models, transferring the 
knowledge they’ve acquired from one domain to another. This becomes 
particularly useful when labeled data is scarce. The CMNV2 model was 
built by combining the modified MobileNetV2 by incorporating five 
additional custom layers aimed at improving feature extraction and 
classification performance specific to deepfake detection. The rationale 
was to enhance the network’s capacity to capture subtle facial manip­
ulations present in deepfakes, which are often missed by standard 
lightweight models like standard MobileNetV2. In this work, Mobile­
NetV2 and CAFFE, which have been previously trained on large datasets, 
are utilized for pre-training. These models are capable of extracting 
general features and patterns, which are then applied to classify real and 
fake face data, as they have already been optimized for classification and 
prediction tasks. Explanation on the role and impact of the additional 
five layers incorporated into the CMNV2 model architecture: 
i. Batch Normalization: This layer standardizes the outputs of the 
preceding layer, improving training efficiency and model stabil­
ity. It ensures the inputs to each layer maintain a mean close to 
0 and variance near 1, enhancing robustness. By computing sta­
tistics across mini-batches, it normalizes activations and includes 
learnable scaling and shifting to increase flexibility.
ii. GlobalAveragePooling2D: After BatchNormalization, a Global­
AveragePooling layer is applied. At the MobileNetV2 stage, this 
layer reduces the spatial dimensions of feature maps by averaging 
values across each map, creating a fixed-length feature vector. 
This step ensures uniformity in feature extraction regardless of 
input image size, while also reducing overfitting and computa­
tional load.
iii. Dense (128 units): This Dense layer contains 128 neurons, 
responsible for refining the extracted features. It compresses 
high-dimensional data into a compact 128-dimensional vector 
while retaining important information. This aids in learning an

Data splitting, CMNV2 model training and testing
The dataset contains 5,000 face images, separated into training and 
testing groups. Section 3 provides a detailed explanation of the dataset’s 
split, which allocates 20% for testing and 80% for training. After loading 
the baseline model, transfer learning is used to generate all 5 layers, as 
explained in Section 4.1. New set of trainable CAFFE layers improved 
facial feature detection and categorization. The model was modified 
after including CAFFE, using the “weight” files and 2 “TXT” files to 
preserve dimensions. With minimal processing time, pre-trained models 
can add bias weights while retaining features. Next, we trained the 
proposed model for 59 epochs at 0.001 using 32 training batches.
To ensure optimal training performance of the CMNV2 model, we 
conducted extensive hyperparameter tuning using a grid search strat­
egy. The tuning process was carried out on a validation subset (20% of 
the dataset) to avoid overfitting and ensure generalization. For the 
learning rate, we tested values in the range 0.001,0.0001,0.1,0.00001 
and observed that a learning rate of 0.001 consistently yielded faster 
convergence and higher validation accuracy. For batch size, we 
experimented with 16, 32 and 64 and found that 32 offered the best 
trade-off between GPU memory usage and gradient stability. The 
number of epochs was selected based on early stopping criteria. We 
monitored the validation loss and stopped training once the loss pla­
teaued or began to increase. The model consistently converged within 
59 epochs, beyond which no significant gain was observed. Addition­
ally, we used the Adam optimizer with which performed better than SGD 
in terms of both convergence speed and final accuracy.

Conclusion
This article introduced a new hybrid CMNV2 model and the results of 
real and deepfake facial images. In this work, 5,000 photo images were 
used. Starting with MobileNetV2, a pre-trained CNN architecture, we 
fine-tuned the DNN by adding 5 layers using transfer learning. Devel­
oped a CAFFE-based deep learning model that increased performance 
and learning speed. Further, with the combination of these 2 models, a 
hybrid CMNV2 model was developed and trained on this dataset with 13 
other deep-learning methods: MobileNet, MobileNetV2, NASNetMobile, 
ResNet50V2, DenseNet121, ResNet101V2, DenseNet169, DenseNet201, 
InceptionV3, ResNet152V2, Xception, InceptionResNetV2, and Effi­
cientNetV2M. The accuracy of 14 evaluated models varied from 76.60% 
to 99.10%. CMNV2 is better than 13 standard methods with high ac­
curacy (99.10%), low error rate (0.90%), better precision, recall, and f1- 
score, with less computational time to classify and detect real and 
deepfake faces. CMNV2 model can be used in various applications, like 
intelligent attendance systems, healthcare and education.
Limitations: The model performed well on still images but struggled 
with real-time video, where accuracy drops due to sudden motion, 
lighting changes, and geographic variations. Rapid participant move­
ment reduces the prediction accuracy, posing a key challenge for video- 
based applications.
