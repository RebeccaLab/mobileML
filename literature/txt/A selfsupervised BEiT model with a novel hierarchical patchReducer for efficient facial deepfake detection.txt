A self-supervised BEiT model with a novel hierarchical 
patchReducer for efficient facial deepfake detection
Aneesa Al Redhaei1 · Salam Fraihat1 · Mohammed Azmi Al-Betar1,2
Artificial Intelligence Review (2025) 58:278
https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-025-11286-8
Abstract
The spread of deepfake technology has become a growing concern, especially with the 
rapid advancement of generative models. This progress has made it increasingly difficult 
to distinguish between real and fake facial videos. This poses serious threats to security, 
privacy, and the spread of misinformation. Despite the existence of deepfake detection 
models, many suffer from high computational costs. This makes them impractical for 
deployment in resource-constrained environments. To address this challenge, this paper 
proposes a solution that accurately identifies deepfakes while reducing computational 
complexity. To achieve this, a deepfake detection system using an improved version of 
a Self-Supervised BEiT, called BEiT-HPR (Hierarchical PatchReducer), is proposed. The 
enhancement adds a Hierarchical PatchReducer layer to reduce the number of patches 
in successive encoder blocks. This reduces computational complexity while maintaining 
high detection accuracy. Additionally, training speed increases by over 50%, while the 
number of parameters is reduced by 63.4%. The BEiT-HPR model was tested using three 
publicly available benchmark datasets: FaceForensics++ (FF++), Celeb-DF, and the Deep­
fake Detection Dataset (DFD). The evaluation results revealed that reducing the model 
complexity by 62.2% allowed the proposed model to achieve an accuracy of 83.92% on 
FF++, 97.59% on Celeb-DF, and 98.25% on DFD. These findings emphasize the impor­
tance of computationally efficient deepfake detection methods that maintain high detection 
accuracy while reducing the burden of heavy computation. Therefore, it offers a scalable 
solution for identifying deepfakes across diverse datasets.
Keywords  Facial deepfake · Vision transformer · Self-supervised learning · 
PatchReducer layer · Computational complexity
1  Introduction
The rapid and exponential advancement of recent technologies in artificial intelligence 
(AI) and deep learning (DL) has led to significant advances in media content creation. This 
includes the development of hyper-realistic artificial media known as “deepfakes”. Deep­

fakes can modify facial features, change speech, or even create entirely false personalities. 
This makes it more challenging to distinguish between authentic and synthetic content. 
The phenomenon gained widespread attention in 2017 with the release of the first deep­
fakes, which used AI to replace the faces of celebrities with inappropriate video content 
(Deshmukh and Wankhade 2020). This marked the beginning of a rise in concerns regarding 
the potential misuse of the technology.
According to the UAE Ministry of AI (AIGov 2021), deepfake technology is becoming 
more powerful and accessible, making it easier to simulate very realistic fake visual con­
tent, including videos and images. These manipulations can harm individuals’ reputations 
and disrupt organizations and national stability by fabricating actions, events, or behaviors. 
Deepfakes can falsely show people involved in activities that never took place. On a local 
level, they can influence public opinion, manipulate communities, and create public agen­
das. If not quickly identified, deepfakes can also damage international relations and pose 
reputational threats to countries.
According to Reuters news (Ulmer and Tong 2023), the number of deepfakes has grown 
rapidly, doubling every six months. In 2023, around 500,000 deepfake videos and audio 
clips were shared globally on social media. By 2025, this number is expected to reach 8 
million, following the same pattern of doubling every six months (Ulmer and Tong 2023). 
As a result, building reliable systems to identify deepfakes is essential to reducing the risks 
they pose, including threats to privacy, security, and societal trust.
In response to these challenges, deepfake detection systems have emerged as crucial 
tools with diverse applications across various sectors (The IoT 2024). In media and jour­
nalism, these systems help verify the authenticity of news footage, combating misinfor­
mation and maintaining journalistic integrity. Law enforcement agencies utilize deepfake 
detection to verify video evidence for criminal investigations, ensuring the reliability of 
digital evidence in legal proceedings. Online platforms employ these technologies for con­
tent moderation, monitoring and removing deepfake content to maintain the credibility of 
user-generated content. In the political sphere, detection systems protect against misleading 
deepfake campaigns, safeguarding democratic processes. Additionally, organizations use 
deepfake detection as part of their cybersecurity measures to guard against identity theft and 
fraud attempts using manipulated media.
In recent years, to address the challenges of detecting deepfakes, several deep learn­
ing techniques have been developed. These methods including advanced approaches like 
long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and hybrid 
models have been used to identify alterations in images and videos (Almars 2021). Among 
these methods, Vision Transformer-based (ViT) (Dosovitskiy et al. 2020) methods are the 
most used (Wang et al. 2024). ViTs are a type of deep learning model that performs well in 
image recognition and classification tasks. ViTs have demonstrated notable success across 
various domains such as healthcare, where they have been applied to medical image pro­
cessing for tasks like skin cancer detection (Pacal et al. 2025; Ozdemir and Pacal 2025) and 
detection of brain stroke (Bayram et al. 2025), and agriculture, where they are used for crop 
disease detection and monitoring (Pacal 2024; Pacal et al. 2024). Their ability to capture 
long-range dependencies in images makes them particularly effective in tasks that require a 
global understanding of the image content. Moreover, they can capture important patterns 
in images, which makes them useful for detecting deepfakes.

However, despite their effectiveness, these methods require large amounts of labeled data 
for training, which is often impractical in deepfake detection (Nguyen et al. 2024; Gerritsen 
2022; Dagar and Vishwakarma 2024). Generating labeled data for a new learning task is 
one of the primary challenges in applying advanced deep learning methods (Li et al. 2024). 
For deepfake detection, collecting sufficient labeled data is both costly and time-consuming. 
This limitation makes ViTs and their improved versions less attractive compared to other 
methods that can perform well with smaller datasets.
Additionally, ViT models rely on the self-attention mechanism, which has quadratic 
complexity relative to the input size. This makes ViTs computationally intensive, especially 
when processing high-resolution images (Qin et al. 2023). As a result, many deepfake detec­
tion studies avoid using vision transformer-based methods or emphasize their high com­
putational cost (Xu et al. 2023; Essa 2024; Maurício et al. 2023). This limitation affects 
the scalability and suitability of ViTs for large-scale training scenarios, making other deep 
learning methods more favorable.
These challenges show the need for new approaches to make ViTs and their improved 
versions more practical for deepfake detection. Reducing their computational cost and data 
requirements, while keeping their performance high. This would make them more useful in 
real-world situations.
To address the challenges of deepfake detection, this paper proposes a self-supervised 
deepfake detection pipeline that integrates several existing techniques in a novel way. The 
key contributions of this research work are as follows:
	
●
Improve deepfake detection accuracy with efficient processing, by the combination of 
techniques such as weight initialization from Self-Supervised pre-trained ImageNet 
models, keyframe extraction, frame balancing, and minimal preprocessing.
	
●
Introduce an Enhanced Vision Transformer (ViT) model, called Bidirectional Encoder 
representation from Image Transformers (BEiT) (Bao et al. 2021). The model incorpo­
rates a PatchReducer layer, an advanced version of the PatchMerger layer (Renggli et al. 
2022), strategically placed within the encoder. This optimization reduces computational 
complexity while maintaining high detection performance.
	
●
Evaluate the proposed model using a cross-data technique, where it is trained on one 
dataset and tested on different datasets. This approach ensures robust generalization by 
preventing overfitting to specific dataset characteristics and validating the model’s ef­
fectiveness across diverse deepfake sources.
	
●
A detailed review of existing deepfake detection methods is provided, with an analysis 
of the gaps in current approaches.
The motivation behind this research is to develop a deepfake detection system that is not 
only accurate but also efficient, making it suitable for real-world applications where com­
putational resources may be limited. By combining these techniques in a novel way, this 
work provides a scalable solution to deepfake detection with minimal data and efficient 
processing.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 summarizes the recent 
studies in the deepfake detection domain. The methodology is presented in Section 3. Sec­
tion 4 presents and discusses the results. The last section states the conclusion and the future 
directions.

2  Literature review
As deepfakes became more popular on social media in 2017, concerns about their potential 
risks grew. Consequently, this led to an increase in demand for appropriate deepfake detec­
tion methods in early 2018. One of the initial techniques developed back then was based 
on distinguishing how frequently a person blinks on deepfake videos compared to real ones 
(Li et al. 2018). Additionally, some early detection methods used statistical tools like pho­
toresponse nonuniformity (PRNU) to spot fake videos. In the work by (Koopman et al. 
2018), PRNU analysis was employed to identify a clear statistical difference in the average 
normalized cross-correlation scores between real and fake videos. However, as generative 
adversarial networks (GANs) models continue to evolve, deepfakes are becoming increas­
ingly realistic. Therefore, more sophisticated detection methods are necessary. Despite 
some machine learning techniques for detecting deepfakes that have been developed, deep 
learning methods are currently the most widely used in the field of deepfake detection (Rana 
et al. 2022; Malik et al. 2022). The deep learning methods include simple traditional deep 
neural networks and more advanced deep learning methods, including CNN-based detec­
tion methods, Transformer-based detection methods, and Hybrid-based detection methods.
2.1  Deep neural network-based detection methods
Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are powerful tools for deepfake detection, as they can ana­
lyze complex patterns in images and videos to identify manipulated content. Various studies 
have demonstrated the effectiveness of DNN-based approaches in this field. For instance, 
Lee and Kim (2021) used a six-layer DNN to analyze changes in visual features, such as 
mean squared error and luminance across video frames, achieving accuracies of 97.39% on 
Face2Face, 95.65% on FaceSwap, and 96.55% on DFDC. Similarly, Sahla Habeeba et al. 
(2021) focused on detecting artifacts in the eyes and teeth regions using a three-layer DNN, 
reporting 92% accuracy on UADFV and 86% on DFD datasets. In another study, Demir 
and Ciftci (2021) utilized eye and gaze features analyzed in spatial, temporal, and spectral 
domains, with a four-layer DNN achieving 92.48% on FaceForensics++, 88.35% on Celeb-
DF, and 99.27% on DeeperForensics. Additionally, Saravana Ram et al. (2023) proposed an 
enhanced Deep Belief Network (DBN) combined with fuzzy clustering for feature extrac­
tion, achieving over 96% accuracy across datasets like Face2Face and 100KFaces.
2.2  Convolutional neural network-based detection methods
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have revolutionized image processing by effectively 
learning and extracting features from visual data. Their hierarchical structure enables them 
to capture complex visual representations, making them highly suitable for deepfake detec­
tion. Early studies employed pre-trained models like VGG, ResNet, Inception, MobileNet, 
DenseNet, and Xception, fine-tuned on deepfake datasets to detect artifacts and inconsisten­
cies introduced during the deepfake generation process (Li and Lyu 2018; Amerini et al. 
2019; Suratkar et al. 2020; Zhang and Liu 2020). More advanced CNN architectures later 
incorporated attention mechanisms and ensemble learning techniques to further enhance 
detection accuracy. One notable CNN-based model is MesoInception (Afchar et al. 2018), 
inspired by InceptionNet, which captures mesoscopic image features. It achieved over 98%

97% accuracy on the Celeb-DF dataset. Lu et al. (2023) incorporated long-distance atten­

features in shallow layers and combines low-level and high-level features with attention

b4 for few-shot scenarios, achieving strong cross-data generalization, with 81.70% accu­
racy on Google DF and 64.40% on DFDC.
2.3  Transformer-based detection methods
Transformers have significantly advanced multiple technological domains, starting with 
their introduction in natural language processing (NLP) by Vaswani et al. (2017). Expand­
ing into computer vision, Vision Transformers (ViTs) treat images as sequences of patches, 
leveraging self-attention to understand spatial relationships (Dosovitskiy et al. 2020). This 
approach has proven highly effective for deepfake detection, often surpassing traditional 
CNNs on extensive datasets (Arshed et al. 2023). Stand-alone ViT models have shown 
exceptional results. For instance, Arshed et  al. (2023) fine-tuned a pre-trained ViT on 
FFHQ and StyleGAN-generated deepfakes, achieving 100% accuracy. Similarly, Ghita 
et al. (2024) customized ViTs for the OpenForensics dataset, attaining 90% accuracy. Thing 
(2023) reported varying accuracies on datasets such as FaceForensics++ (75.73%), Google 
DFD (87.20%), and DeeperForensics (99.73%). Enhanced ViT architectures like the Swin 
Transformer (Liu et al. 2021) have further improved detection. For example, Mishra et al. 
(2024) achieved 97.91% accuracy on Celeb-DF, and Thing (2023) explored both Swin 
Transformer and BEiT frameworks, yielding similar success. Computationally efficient

Advanced approaches have incorporated spatial and temporal features. Ramadhani

achieving over 90% accuracy on FaceForensics++. Khormali and Yuan (2022) introduced

2.4  Hybrid-based detection methods
Hybrid-based detection methods combine multiple computational models, enhancing deep­
fake detection by integrating neural networks, convolutional layers, and transformer archi­
tectures. For example, (Mirzayev and DİBEKLİOĞLU 2024) proposed combining CNNs 
with LSTMs, using motion magnification-inspired feature manipulation to enhance frame 
representations, achieving 86% accuracy on Celeb-DF. (Heidari et al. 2024) introduced a 
federated learning framework using SegCaps, CNNs, and capsule networks, significantly 
improving performance and achieving accuracy rates above 97% on datasets like CelebDF 
and FaceForensics++. (Tolosana et  al. 2022) applied fusion techniques at both system 
and facial region levels, achieving an AUC score above 99% across three datasets. (Lin 
et al. 2023) integrated multi-scale convolutions and a vision transformer, reaching 99.8% 
accuracy on Celeb-DF and 88.47% on DFDC. Similarly, (Wang et  al. 2023) combined 
convolutional pooling and re-attention mechanisms, yielding 92.11% accuracy on FaceFo­
rensics++. (Khormali and Yuan 2024) employed a Vision Transformer-based feature extrac­
tor and a Graph Convolutional Network, achieving 99.47% accuracy on Celeb-DF. Finally, 
(Kavthekar et al. 2023) introduced a hybrid model using Masked Autoencoder ConvNeXt 
and Multiscale Vision Transformer, achieving accuracy ranging from 80.22% to 98.1%. 
These hybrid approaches offer effective solutions by leveraging the strengths of multiple 
models.

3  Methodology
The proposed system leverages state-of-the-art ViT technology, enhanced through self-
supervised learning methods. Central to this system is the utilization of a pre-trained BEiT 
model, which is further refined to enhance its deepfake detection capabilities. As illustrated 
in Fig. 1, the system operates in three primary stages: data preparation, model training, and 
performance evaluation.
3.1  Datasets
The proposed deepfake detection system is trained on three well-known benchmark datas­
ets: FaceForensics++ (FF++) (Rossler et al. 2019; Li et al. 2019), Celeb-DF (Li et al. 2020), 
and the DeepFake Detection Dataset (DFD) (Dufour and Gully 2019). These datasets are 
selected for their diversity in deepfake generation techniques, video resolutions, and real-
world scenarios, which contribute to the robustness and generalizability of the model.

FF++ contains 5,000 fake videos and 1,000 real videos, with the real videos sourced 
from YouTube. The fake videos are generated using various facial manipulation techniques, 
including DeepFakes (DF), Face2Face (F2F), FaceSwap (FS), NeuralTextures (NT), and 
FaceShifter (FSh), which encompass both deep learning-based and computer graphics-based 
methods. The dataset also includes three types of video quality: Raw (no compression), 
High Quality (HQ) with low compression, and Low Quality (LQ) with high compression. 
For the current study, HQ videos are used to balance memory usage and video quality. The 
video resolutions in FF++ range from 480p to 1080p, with 480p being the most common. 
The diversity of manipulation techniques and video resolutions in FF++ makes it a valuable 
benchmark for deepfake detection research (Rossler et al. 2019; Li et al. 2019).
Celeb-DF consists of 5,639 deepfake videos generated from 590 original video sequences 
of 59 celebrities, with each video typically lasting around 13 s at 30 frames per second. The 
real videos are sourced from YouTube interviews and reflect a variety of genders, ages, and 
ethnicities. The deepfake videos are generated using an advanced synthesis method that 
swaps faces among the 59 individuals. To improve the visual fidelity of the manipulated 
faces, the dataset creators enhance the resolution of the deepfake faces to 256×256 pixels 
using advanced encoder-decoder models. This high-resolution manipulation increases the 
difficulty of distinguishing fake videos from real ones, making Celeb-DF a challenging 
dataset for deepfake detection (Li et al. 2020).
The DeepFake Detection Dataset (DFD) was created by Google and later merged into 
the FF++ dataset. It includes 360 real and 3,068 deepfake videos, featuring a wide range 
of ethnicities, lighting conditions, and scene complexities. The dataset was generated using 
undisclosed deepfake technologies, which adds to its complexity and makes it less com­
monly used in prior research. The variety in the DFD dataset further contributes to the 
model’s ability to generalize across different types of deepfake manipulations and environ­
mental conditions (Dufour and Gully 2019).
3.2  Data preprocessing
This section explains the preprocessing steps: data splitting, frame extraction, frame balanc­
ing, face detection, and face normalization with resizing.
3.2.1  Data splitting
The dataset is divided into three subsets: training, validation, and testing. The training set is 
used to train the model. During the training phase, the validation set helps tune hyperparam­
eters and prevent overfitting. The testing set is used for final evaluation.
Each of the three datasets is split into non-overlapping training, validation, and test sets. 
For the FF++ dataset, the provided splits are used, while for the other datasets, the videos are 
divided to allocate approximately 70% for training, 10% for validation, and 20% for testing. 
This non-overlapping division is essential for ensuring that the model’s performance evalu­
ation is unbiased and reflects its ability to generalize to unseen data. Generalizability, in 
this context, refers to the model’s ability to accurately detect deepfakes on videos or frames 
that it has never encountered during training. By isolating the testing set, the model’s per­
formance is assessed under conditions that mimic real-world scenarios, where the deepfake 
detection system must handle entirely new and unseen content. Furthermore, the use of a

3.2.2  Frame extraction
Videos consist of a sequence of images called frames. Frame extraction is a process of 
selecting individual frames from a video sequence. This work extracts keyframes, or 
I-frames, from real and fake videos. I-frames are important because they hold the most 
visual information and often mark significant changes or transitions in the video, such as 
movements, expressions, or events (referred to as "actions") (Wang et al. 2023). Analyzing 
I-frames helps detect subtle alterations that may not appear in other frames. The extraction 
process identifies I-frames and feeds them into the detection model, reducing computational 
load and improving the detection of deepfake artifacts. Collecting more I-frames enhances 
the accuracy and reliability of the system. Figure 2 shows an example of I-frame extraction 
from an 8-second video at 30 fps.

3.2.3  Frame Balancing

reducing bias toward any specific class. A noticeable imbalance between fake and real video 
classes is denoted in Table 3, where the fake class contains significantly more frames. To 
address this issue, more samples were added to the minority class (real videos) by extracting 
extra random frames. This balanced the number of real and fake frames, helping the model 
learn from an evenly distributed dataset and improving its ability to distinguish between 
real and fake content. To ensure the authenticity and diversity of real data, methods like data 
augmentation, oversampling, or synthetic data generation were avoided. These techniques 
can introduce artificial variations or artifacts (e.g., rotations, flips, or brightness adjust­
ments) that do not accurately reflect real-world scenarios, which could affect the model 
performance. Instead, the real class was balanced by the extraction of additional frames

3.2.4  Face region extraction
After extracting and balancing the frames, face detection is applied to isolate facial regions 
in each frame. This helps the model focus on the face, improving its ability to detect manip­
ulations. The Dlib-HOG face detector (Dalal and Triggs 2005) is used due to its balance of 
accuracy and efficiency. It is an image-based method in which discriminative features are 
generally learned from training images and applied dimensionality reduction for efficiency 
(Kumar et al. 2019). Unlike Eigenfaces-based detection assumes that facial images lie in a 
linear subspace (Ho et al. 2024). This assumption often fails in real-world scenarios with 
variations in pose, lighting, and facial expressions (Ho et al. 2024). Image-based methods 
like Dlib-HOG handle these variations more effectively. Also, a recent study by Abdullah 
and Ali (2024) found that Dlib-HOG outperforms other methods, such as MTCNN, for face 
detection in deepfake detection.

from the frames of the video.

3.3  The proposed deepfake detection model
After the completion of the data preprocessing pipeline, which ensures that high-quality, 
balanced, and facially focused frames are fed into the system. The next stage involves the 
building of a deepfake detection model. In this section, the proposed detection model is 
introduced, which is built upon a pre-trained self-supervised BEiT architecture (Bao et al. 
2021).
3.3.1  Pre-trained Self-supervised BEiT
The BEiT model was first trained in a self-supervised way using the ImageNet-22k dataset. 
During this phase, the model learned to reconstruct missing parts of images, allowing it to 
understand image structures without needing labeled data. Afterward, it was fine-tuned for 
image classification using the labeled ImageNet-1k dataset, as illustrated in Fig. 4.
In this study, instead of training the BEiT model from scratch, the pre-trained weights 
from the fine-tuned ImageNet-1k model were used as the initial weights for the proposed 
BEiT-HPR model. This approach improves the performance of the training process because 
the model begins with learned features that help it understand the data better. It also speeds 
up training, as the model does not need to learn basic patterns from the beginning, allowing 
it to focus on task-specific features.
This approach is particularly advantageous for datasets and tasks where labeled data is 
limited, as the pre-trained model has already acquired a broad understanding of general 
image features from a large-scale dataset.

3.3.2  The enhanced BEiT architecture: the BEiT-HPR model
The enhanced BEiT model represents a significant contribution to the advancement of 
vision transformer-based architectures that are known for their high computational com­
plexity. The core concept revolves around enhancing the BEiT by strategically reducing 
the complexity of the model while maintaining the same performance. This is achieved 
through the introduction of a PatchReducer Layer. This layer is an enhanced version of the 
PatchMerger concept in (Renggli et al. 2022), which is inserted at specific positions within 
the BEiT encoder blocks. The PatchReducer is used to follow hierarchical structures seen 
in architectures like CNNs and the hierarchical vision transformer proposed in (Liu et al. 
2021). Unlike the original BEiT, which uses a fixed number of image patches throughout all 
layers, this approach progressively reduces the number of patches. As a result, it mitigates 
the computational load while maintaining the same performance. Figure 5 shows the archi­
tecture of the BEiT with the Hierarchical PatchReducer (BEiT-HPR) model and details the 
PatchReducer Layer.

1.	 Patch Embedding The input image is first split into small patches. These patches are 
then flattened and converted into vectors for further processing. Also, a special patch, 
[S], is added to represent the entire image globally. This patch collects information from 
all patches throughout the model and is important for the final classification. Then, all 
these patches, along with [S], are embedded and passed to the encoder blocks.
2.	 BEiT Encoder The BEiT encoder is composed of alternating layers of Relative Multi-
Head Self-Attention (RMHSA) (Bao et al. 2021) and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) 
(Dosovitskiy et al. 2020) blocks. Layer Normalization (LN) (Lei Ba et al. 2016) is 
applied before each RMHSA and MLP layers, while LayerScale (LS) (Touvron et al. 
2021) and residual connections are incorporated after each RMHSA and MLP layers. 
As shown in Fig. 5, the PatchReducer Layer is added between the encoder blocks in 
a hierarchy way. The first four encoder blocks maintain the original patch resolution 
with N patches. This is critical as the early layers are responsible for capturing fine-
grained, low-level features such as edges, textures, and small details. After the first 
PatchReducer layer is applied, the number of patches is reduced by a factor of 12, 
resulting in N/12 patches. This occurs at the start of the fifth encoder block. These 
blocks focus more on mid-level abstractions, such as parts of faces or larger patterns. 
For N = 196 input patches, a PatchReducer layer with 16 output patches (M = 16) is 
applied after the fourth encoder block, effectively reducing the 196 patches by a factor 
of 12. The second PatchReducer layer further reduces the patch number by a factor of 
24, leaving N/24 patches for the subsequent layers. At this depth, the network begins to 
focus on higher-level representations and global context. For N = 196 input patches, a 
PatchReducer layer with 8 output patches (M = 8) is applied after the eighth encoder 
block, effectively reducing the 196 patches by a factor of 24. The final PatchReducer 
layer is applied, reducing the number of patches to N/48. These final blocks deal with 
the most abstract representations, capturing the overall structure and semantics of the 
input image. For N = 196 input patches, a PatchReducer layer with 4 output patches 
(M = 4) is applied after the tenth encoder block, effectively reducing the 196 patches 
by a factor of 48. By progressively reducing the patches, the model balances perfor­
mance and efficiency, focusing on the most meaningful features as it goes deeper into 
the network. More discussion about this hierarchy reduction is provided in Sect. 4.
3.	 Classification Layer At the end of the encoders, the [S] patch is extracted and passed 
to a classification layer. This layer is responsible for determining the authenticity of the 
input image, classifying it as either real or fake based on its features.

PatchReducer reduces the computational load by decreasing the number of patches pro­
cessed, leading to notable speed improvements (40-60%) while preserving performance 
comparable to the original model. The following sections provide a detailed breakdown of 
each operation within the PatchReducer layer, as illustrated in Fig. 5. 
1.	 Splitting the Special Patch[S] At the beginning of the PatchReducer process, the spe­
cial token [S]( representing global information about the image) is separated from the 
main patch set, as shown in Fig. 5. This special token carries information about the 
entire input image and will be reintroduced after the processing of the patches.
2.	 Layer Normalization The remaining patches are passed through a Layer Normal­
ization step, as illustrated in Fig. 5. This step ensures that the input patches are stan­
dardized in terms of scale and distribution. It helps the learned transformations in the 
following steps to be applied evenly across all patches, operating along the embedding 
dimension D. This process stabilizes training and improves performance by reducing 
internal covariate shifts.
3.	 Multiplication by Learnable Matrix(RD×M) After normalization, the patches are 
multiplied by a learnable matrix, as seen in Fig. 5, initialized with a Glorot uniform ini­
tializer. It projects the normalized patches into a new space. This matrix has dimensions 
D × M, where M is the reduced number of patches, and D is the embedding dimen­
sion. The learnable matrix enables the model to determine how to combine the patches, 
deciding which spatial regions are most important for the next layer.
4.	 Transpose Operation The next step, shown in Fig. 5, involves transposing the result­
ing matrix. This reshapes the data to align the dimensions for matrix operations. Trans­
posing prepares the model for an attention mechanism that evaluates the importance of 
each patch.
5.	 Scale & Softmax Application To combine patches, the transposed matrix is scaled by 
1
√
D before applying the softmax function, as depicted in Fig. 5. This scaling, similar to 
the scaled dot-product attention in transformers, prevents gradient vanishing or explod­
ing, especially for large D. It stabilizes the softmax operation and improves training 
performance. The softmax function normalizes the patch values into probabilities that 
sum to 1. This helps the model focus on important patches by assigning them higher 
weights while reducing the impact of less relevant regions.
6.	 Multiplication by Normalized Patches After calculating the softmax weights, the 
model multiplies them with the original normalized patches, as shown in Fig. 5. This 
step merges the patches based on their importance, as determined by the softmax 
weights. The resulting patches provide a compact representation of the most important 
information from the original set.
7.	 Concatenation with the Special Patch[S] The reduced set of patches is then concate­
nated with the special patch [S], as shown in Fig. 5. This ensures that global information 
about the image is preserved for the next layers. Concatenating [S] allows the model to 
maintain a complete understanding of the image while reducing the number of patches 
for more efficient computation.

3.4  Complexity analysis
To examine the computational complexity of the proposed BEiT model, the number of float­

metric that calculates the total arithmetic operations needed to train a model, where a higher 
FLOPs value reflects greater time complexity (Cong and Yang 2023). The following lines 
demonstrate the mathematical modeling used to calculate the total number of FLOPs in 
transformer-based models as stated in (Han et al. 2021).

account. Accordingly, the FLOPs for these three projection operations are calculated as fol­

resulting in an observed accuracy of 98.20%, the highest among the tested configurations.

of self-learning weights in improving the model’s performance for deepfake detection.

6  Ablation study of patchreducer layer
The ablation study examines the impact of the PatchReducer layer on the performance of 
the BEiT-HPR model. By comparing results with and without the PatchReducer layer across 
each dataset, this analysis highlights its contribution to both detection accuracy and com­
putational efficiency.
Table 10 summarizes the ablation study on the complexity of the PatchReducer layer. 
The complexity of each model is measured in FLOPs (floating-point operations), calculated 
using the method described in section 3.4 and Eq. 9. Additionally, the table analyzes the 
number of positional embedding parameters (Pos_Embeddings) affected by the PatchRe­
ducer layer.
The original BEiT architecture demonstrates a computational complexity of 17.6245 
billion FLOPs, reflecting the high resource demand typically associated with transformer-
based models. In contrast, the proposed BEiT-HPR, which incorporates the PatchReducer 
layer at strategic encoder stages, achieves a drastic reduction in complexity to 6.6553 billion 
FLOPs. This represents an approximately 62.2% decrease in computational demand while 
maintaining competitive detection performance, as discussed in previous sections.
In addition, the proposed BEiT-HPR model reduces positional embedding
(Pos_Embeddings) parameters by 63.4%, from 105,408 to 38,592. Positional embed­
dings are directly tied to the number of patches, unlike other parameters linked to the 
model’s architecture and hidden dimensions. This reduction improves parameter efficiency 
while preserving the model’s ability to encode spatial information.
The large decrease in FLOPs and number of positional embedding parameters highlights 
how effectively the PatchReducer layer simplifies the model’s operations. By reducing 
patches in the later stages of the encoder, the BEiT-HPR minimizes the size of the data 
being processed, which lowers the number of calculations needed.

8  Conclusion
This work introduced an efficient and scalable system for detecting deepfake faces in vid­
eos. The proposed BEiT-HPR model incorporates a PatchReducer layer, which reduces 
the number of patches processed at different stages of the encoder. This approach lowers 
computational complexity by 62.2% while maintaining high detection accuracy. The model 
achieved impressive results in in-data evaluations, with accuracies of 83.92% on FF++, 
97.59% on Celeb-DF, and 98.25% on DFD. It also performed well in cross-data evaluations, 
demonstrating its ability to handle data from different sources.
Despite these achievements, the research identified key limitations. The PatchReducer 
layer was only tested within the BEiT architecture. Its effectiveness on other transformer-
based models has not been evaluated. Moreover, similar to the other deep learning models, 
the proposed method is limited in its ability to generalize to unseen datasets during cross-
data evaluation. The model is trained on specific types of manipulations within the dataset, 
which allows it to perform well on similar data. However, when tested on new or unknown 
data, its performance decreases. This is a common challenge in detection systems as the 
unseen dataset can differ significantly from those in the training data due to variations in 
video compression, lighting conditions, and manipulation techniques. This limitation high­
lights the need for models that can adapt to a wider range of deepfake datasets without 
retraining. Another limitation is that the system is designed for binary classification, iden­
tifying whether a face is real or fake, but it cannot detect specific types of manipulations.

Additionally, the model focuses only on faces and does not analyze other manipulated ele­
ments in a video, such as backgrounds or objects.
Future work should focus on addressing these limitations. We aim to further optimize 
the PatchReducer layers to enhance efficiency without compromising detection accuracy. 
Potential improvements include incorporating adaptive patch reduction, multi-scale pro­
cessing, and learnable patch selection. These techniques could enable dynamic adjustment 
of patch reduction based on input complexity, ensuring better feature retention while reduc­
ing computational costs. In addition, exploring the integration of PatchReducer into other 
transformer-based architectures could provide further insights into its generalizability and 
effectiveness in deepfake detection.
While this work focuses on improving deepfake detection efficiency, ethical consider­
ations are also important. Ensuring privacy, fairness, and responsible use is crucial as these 
models are used in real-world applications. This model can be deployed in various envi­
ronments where data privacy and bias matter. Future developments should follow ethical
