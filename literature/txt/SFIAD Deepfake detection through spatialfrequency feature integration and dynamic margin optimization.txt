Abstract
The rapid advancement of generative models has profoundly transformed the field of digi­
tal content creation, bringing unprecedented opportunities for media generation. However, 
the widespread adoption of this technology has also led to the emergence of highly realis­
tic fake facial images and videos, which pose significant threats to public trust and societal 
security. To address the challenges of deepfake detection, this paper proposes a novel 
method based on Spatial-Frequency Feature Integration (SFFI), which effectively identi­
fies fake content by combining spatial and frequency features of images. Additionally, 
to tackle the issue of class imbalance in the datasets, we propose an Authenticity-Aware 
Margin Loss (AAML). This loss function dynamically adjusts the decision boundary to 
enhance the model’s ability to recognize minority class samples. The proposed method 
was trained and evaluated on four challenging datasets: FaceForensics++, Celeb-DF v1, 
Celeb-DF v2, and the DeepFake Detection Challenge Preview, and compared against ten 
state-of-the-art methods. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method con­
sistently outperforms all existing approaches across all datasets.
Keywords  Generative models · Deepfake · Deepfake detection · SFFI · AAML
1  Introduction
Deep generative models (Goodfellow et al. 2020; Karras et al. 2019) have made break­
throughs in digital content creation. They could generate many fake face images and videos 
(Chan et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2021; Thies et al. 2019) but simultaneously impose a serious 
challenge to the authenticity of information, especially in terms of privacy invasion and 
political speech manipulation. To this end, deepfake detection technology has emerged.
Early Deepfake detection methods (Dang et al. 2020; Li et al. 2020; Nguyen et al. 2019; 
Zi et al. 2020; Afchar et al. 2018; Rossler et al. 2019) primarily relied on convolutional neu­
ral networks (CNNs) to extract local facial features. While effective in specific tasks, their

applicability to Deepfake detection remains limited. These approaches often overlook the 
complex global information and frequency domain features inherent in Deepfake images, 
making them inadequate for handling sophisticated forgeries. To address these limitations, 
recent studies have shifted focus to identifying specific patterns in forged images, such as 
noise features (Gu et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2017), local textures (Chen et al. 2021; Gu et al. 
2022; Zhao et al. 2021), and frequency information (Li et al. 2021; Qian et al. 2020), to 
enhance detection accuracy. However, relying on patterns learned from training data renders 
these methods significantly constrained when facing novel forgery techniques.
Currently, the focus of deepfake detection research has gradually shifted to improving 
model generalization. For example, Yan et al. (2023) proposed a decoupling framework that 
enhances generalization by separating forgery traces from content information. However, 
this method can still capture specific forgery patterns, limiting its adaptability to unknown 
forgery techniques. Yan et al. (2024) proposed the LSAD method based on a teacher-student 
architecture, where the teacher module learns domain-specific features through domain loss, 
and the student module enhances generalization. However, the performance of LSAD is 
highly dependent on the diversity of the training data, and its generalization ability is limited 
when data is insufficient. Moreover, these methods operate over the entire feature space, 
making them vulnerable to irrelevant factors such as background and identity, which can 
reduce detection accuracy.
To address this issue, this paper proposes a novel detection framework, Spatial-Frequency 
Feature Integration and Dynamic Margin Optimization (SFIAD), whose overall architecture 
is illustrated in Fig. 1, consisting of four key components: Spatial-Frequency Feature Inte­
gration (SFFI), a pre-trained Xception model, a frame-level classifier, and the Authenticity-
Aware Margin Loss (AAML) loss function. The SFFI method combines the original color 
image with frequency domain information, mitigating cross-image inconsistencies caused 
by variations in brightness and contrast. The normalization process enhances the model’s 
sensitivity to structural information, significantly improving its robustness against various 
forgery techniques. We employ the ImageNet-pre-trained Xception network to extract hier­
archical features capturing complex image patterns.
The frame-level classifier processes each frame to extract key features, laying the founda­
tion for video content understanding and improved classification accuracy. By dynamically 
adjusting class-specific decision boundaries according to each class’s sample distribution, 
the AAML loss function increases weight allocation to real images and encourages focused 
model attention on discriminative features, thus significantly enhancing classification per­
formance, especially on highly imbalanced datasets.

2  Related work
2.1  Deepfake generation
Traditional image and video forgery techniques typically involve intercepting source con­
tent and embedding target objects, using simple pixel-level operations to create fake images 
(Wang et al. 2022; Rossler et al. 2019). However, with the rapid advancement of deep 
learning (Muksimova et al. 2024), deepfake technology has made significant breakthroughs, 
greatly enhancing the realism of forged content and making it nearly indistinguishable. 
Early forgery video generation methods relied on autoencoders (D’Avino et al. 2017) for 
image reconstruction, but these methods were typically limited to simple reconstruction of 
input data, resulting in lower-quality fake images.

The advent of Generative Adversarial Networks (Karras et al. 2019; Li et al. 2018) has 
revolutionized the generation of forged content. Through the adversarial training between 
the generator and the discriminator, GANs can generate fake images or videos that are visu­
ally more realistic and rich in detail. In recent years, GAN-based deepfake technology has 
made significant progress in areas such as face swapping, voice synthesis, and facial expres­
sion manipulation. The introduction of Conditional GANs (Odena et al. 2017; Mirza 2014) 
and Cycle Consistent Networks (Zhu et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2018; Huang et al. 2018) has 
particularly improved the quality and consistency of generated images and videos.
However, as technology advances, the generation of fake content has become increas­
ingly complex, especially with the emergence of diffusion models (Ho et al. 2020; Saharia 
et al. 2022). Unlike GANs, diffusion models provide more refined control over the gen­
eration process by simulating a step-by-step denoising process, achieving new heights in 
the detail and consistency of forged content. This technological advancement has further 
enhanced the realism of fake images and videos, posing new challenges for traditional forg­
ery detection methods. Therefore, in response to the growing complexity of forgery tech­
nology, the deepfake detection field urgently requires more powerful and flexible detection 
methods.
2.2  Deepfake detection
In the field of deep forgery detection, the generalization ability of the model, especially the 
adaptability to different forgery techniques and datasets, remains a key challenge. Existing 
research focuses on two main directions: frame-level forgery detection and video-level forg­
ery detection. Frame-level forgery detection focuses on extracting forgery features from a 
single image frame, while video-level forgery detection identifies forged content by analyz­
ing the temporal relationship between video frames.
In frame-level forgery detection, Chen et al. (2022) proposed a detection method com­
bining self-supervised learning and adversarial data augmentation, effectively enhancing the 
model’s adaptability to unknown forgery techniques by generating diverse forged images. 
Liu et al. (2021) introduced the SPSL method, which combines spatial domain and phase 
spectrum information, utilizing shallow networks to focus on local texture features, sig­
nificantly improving the model’s generalization ability across different datasets. Luo et al. 
(2021) further enhanced the model’s cross-database consistency by incorporating high-fre­
quency noise features with attention mechanisms. Dong et al. (2023) proposed a detection 
method that does not rely on identity information, focusing on local forgery features, thus 
improving the model’s adaptability to unknown forgery techniques. Huang et al. (2023) 
introduced explicit and implicit identity loss, proposing a framework driven by implicit 
identities, which effectively enhanced the model’s generalization ability.
Additionally, feature disentangling learning and reconstruction-based learning methods 
have shown significant potential in deepfake detection. Yan et al. (2023) proposed a multi-task 
disentangling framework combining conditional decoders and contrastive regularization, 
which effectively extracts common features across different forgery techniques, improving 
detection accuracy. Liang et al. (2022) introduced a framework that constrains feature inde­
pendence to suppress the interference of content information, enhancing the identification 
of forgery traces. In terms of network design, Dang et al. (2020) proposed a novel atten­
tion mechanism and demonstrated its effectiveness on large-scale forgery datasets. Zhao

3.1  The intuitive idea behind our method
3.1.1  An intuitive understanding of SFFI
Forgery detection should not be limited to spatial domain analysis of images but should also 
delve into the frequency domain to reveal subtle features of forged content. Forged images 
often exhibit unnatural high-frequency noise and abnormal frequency distributions in the 
frequency domain, which are difficult to capture through traditional spatial domain analysis. 
Our intuition is that by combining spatial domain features with frequency domain features, 
we can more comprehensively capture the subtle differences in forged images.
3.1.2  An intuitive understanding of AAML
Class imbalance is a common issue in deep fake detection, where the number of real sam­
ples is significantly lower than that of fake samples, leading to a reduced ability of the model 
to recognize real samples during training. To address this issue, a loss function is proposed 
that dynamically adjusts the decision boundary based on class imbalance.

tion, we propose the AAML loss function. This loss function dynamically adjusts the deci­
sion boundary based on the sample distribution of each class, assigning a larger margin to 
real images, thereby increasing their weight during training and encouraging the model 
to focus more on recognizing real images. This design significantly improves classifica­
tion performance on imbalanced datasets. To illustrate the steps of the proposed method, 
Algorithm 1 shows the overall process of our deepfake detection algorithm. The current 
framework focuses on frame-level detection. When conducting subsequent research on 
video-level detection, a spatio-temporal feature processing module will be added based on 
the existing one. This module will be placed between the SFFI module and the pre-trained 
Xception model. It is responsible for extracting the spatio-temporal features of video frame 
sequences. For instance, it can use the optical flow method to obtain the speed and direc­
tion information of facial movements. Then, these spatio-temporal features will be fused 
with the single-frame features extracted by the SFFI module and input into the pre-trained 
Xception model for more advanced feature extraction and analysis, thereby achieving more 
accurate detection of video-level deepfakes.

3.3.2  Pretrained-xception
We utilize a pre-trained convolutional neural network (CNN) module to extract spatial fea­
tures from each frame. The Xception model, pre-trained on ImageNet, is selected, with its 
final fully connected (FC) layer removed to serve as a feature extractor. To accommodate a 
different number of input channels, we modify the weight-loading process of Xception to 
handle an input format distinct from the original model. After processing, the output feature 
map from the pre-trained Xception model has a size of m × 2048, where m represents the 
number of frames and 2048 is the feature dimension.

3.3.3  Frame-level classifier
The features from the Pretrained-Xception model are fed into the frame-level classifier. It

nected (FC) layer. In our implementation, the length of the input video is set to 32 frames 
(more details provided in Sect. 4), and the size of the input frame is 256 × 256.
3.4  AAML
In the task of deepfake detection, class imbalance is a critical issue. With fake samples dom­
inating the dataset and authentic samples being relatively scarce, this imbalance weakens 
the model’s ability to recognize the minority class (authentic samples). To address this chal­
lenge, we propose the AAML loss function. By dynamically adjusting classification bound­
aries, AAML significantly enhances the model’s ability to distinguish authentic images, 
particularly in scenarios with severe data imbalance.
The core mechanism of AAML is to dynamically adjust the classification margin based 
on the sample count of each class, effectively mitigating the impact of class imbalance on 
model training. Specifically, for the class with fewer samples (authentic samples), AAML 
assigns a larger margin my, while for the class with more samples (fake samples), it sets a 
smaller margin my. This differential margin design encourages the model to focus more on 
the characteristics of authentic images during classification. Given the sample count ny for 
class y, the margin my is calculated as follows:

4  Experiments
4.1  Settings
Datasets Our method has been evaluated on four challenging datasets, including FaceFo­
rensics++ (FF++) (Rossler et al. 2019), CelebDF-v1 (CDFv1) (Li et al. 2020), CelebDF-v2 
(CDFv2) (Li et al. 2020), and DeepFake Detection Challenge Preview (DFDC-P) (Dol­
hansky et al. 2019). FF++ is currently the most widely used dataset for synthetic video 
forgery, consisting of 1000 authentic videos and corresponding manipulated videos gen­
erated through four distinct manipulation techniques: Deepfakes (DF), Face2Face (F2F), 
FaceSwap (FS), and Neural Textures (NT). The FF++ dataset also offers three versions 
based on compression levels: original, light compression (c23), and heavy compression 
(c40). Following the convention of previous studies, we have selected the light compression 
version (c23) of the FF++ dataset for our experiments. This dataset is primarily utilized for 
model training, with the others reserved for testing purposes.
Evaluation metrics Consistent with prior studies (Yan et al. 2023, 2024), our evalu­
ation employs established metrics: Accuracy (ACC), Average Precision (AP), and the 
Area Under Curve (AUC), while incorporating F1-score and computational efficiency 
(GFLOPS/Params) for comprehensive performance characterization.
Implementation details The experiment configuration parameters are given in Table 
1. All methods use the same training configuration, including image size, optimizer 
choice, learning rate, and data augmentation strategy. During the data processing phase, 
we selected 32 frames from each video sample for training and testing. Additionally, 
we efficiently executed facial detection, cropping, and alignment tasks using the DLIB 
library, standardizing the image dimensions to 256×256 pixels. In the training process, we 
employed the Adam optimization algorithm with a learning rate of 0.0002 and uniformly 
adopted a batch size of 32. To enhance the robustness of the model, we implemented a 
range of data augmentation strategies during the training phase, including image compres­
sion, horizontal flipping, rotation, Gaussian blurring, and random adjustments to bright­
ness and contrast. All experiments were conducted on NVIDIA GTX 3090 GPUs, ensuring 
ample computational resources and consistency in the experimental environment. To 
ensure a fair comparison, all experiments are conducted within the DeepfakeBench (Yan 
et al. 2023). All our experimental settings follow the default settings of the benchmarks.

LSDA (Yan et al. 2024) adopts a teacher-student architecture, where the teacher module 
learns domain-specific features through domain loss, while the student network enhances 
generalization through feature distillation. However, its performance heavily depends on 
the diversity of training data. When data is insufficient, LSDA struggles to capture the dis­
tribution of forgery features effectively, limiting its adaptability to unknown forgery types. 
Furthermore, LSDA involves complex latent space operations, has a long training time, and 
is highly sensitive to hyperparameters, increasing training difficulty and uncertainty.
UCF (Yan et al. 2023) uses a multi-task learning strategy and conditional decoder to 
decouple image features, extracting general forgery features to improve detection general­
ization. However, UCF has two main issues: first, the computational complexity of feature 
decoupling is high, especially on large-scale datasets; second, the decision process relies on 
a complex decoupling and contrastive learning mechanism, resulting in poor interpretability 
and making it difficult to explain the detection rationale to users.

SFIAD: Deepfake detection through spatial-frequency feature integration…
SRM (Luo et al. 2021) and F3-Net (Qian et al. 2020) methods distinguish original images 
from forged ones by leveraging image frequency components and perform reasonably well 
when dealing with known forgery patterns. However, these two methods heavily rely on 
the forgery features learned from the training samples. Due to their inability to extract new 
features, their performance significantly declines when encountering unknown forgery pat­
terns. In comparison, our proposed SFIAD model has significant advantages. First, SFIAD 
fuses spatial and frequency domain features, broadening the capture range of deepfake fea­
tures and effectively improving detection capabilities. Second, SFIAD employs Fast Fourier 
Transform (FFT) to perform spatial spectrum synthesis analysis, overcoming the limitations 
of traditional methods that rely solely on high-pass filtering or specific frequency features.
CORE (Ni et al. 2022) and Recce (Cao et al. 2022) focus on different detection algo­
rithms, including designing specific loss functions and employing reconstruction learning. 
However, these detectors can still be affected by irrelevant factors such as race, gender, or 
identity, making it challenging to eliminate these confounding influences.
FWA (Li and Lyu 2018) method detects fake videos by identifying resolution inconsis­
tencies in face transformations. However, it has two notable limitations: first, it lacks the 
ability to capture irrelevant transformation artifacts, which leads to a decline in detection 
performance; second, it relies on specific image processing techniques to generate nega­
tive samples, making it prone to overfitting to certain forgery patterns and resulting in poor 
generalization.
RFM (Wang and Deng 2021) method guides the detector to focus on more representative 
forged regions through forged attention maps and a suspicious forgery elimination module, 
improving detection capability across various forgery techniques. However, RFM has two 
limitations: first, it heavily relies on the quality of training data, with noise or mislabel­
ing significantly affecting model performance; second, its attention mechanism is prone to 
interference from specific forgery patterns, leading to an overemphasis on local features and 
reducing the model’s generalization ability.
Finally, MesoNet (Afchar et al. 2018) and Xception (Rossler et al. 2019) are pure convo­
lutional network structures that lack data augmentation, feature disentanglement, or utiliza­
tion of frequency information. Their performance significantly declines when dealing with 
unknown forged videos.

demonstrating its robustness in handling unknown forgery patterns.

To evaluate the impact of the SFFI and AAML modules on the SFIAD method, we per­
formed ablation experiments by removing each module individually as well as both mod­
ules simultaneously. The results are summarized in Table 5, which reports accuracy (ACC),

Removing the SFFI module caused significant performance degradation: on Celeb-DF-
V1, ACC decreased from 78.06% to 69.71%, AUC from 88.40% to 81.56%, and AP from 
92.45% to 86.86%; on Celeb-DF-V2, ACC decreased from 75.19% to 71.36%, AUC from 
79.00% to 76.45%, and AP from 86.58% to 83.29%; on DFDCP, ACC decreased from 
71.43% to 66.51%, AUC from 75.88% to 74.82%, and AP from 83.96% to 83.23%.
Similarly, removing the AAML module also led to performance degradation: on Celeb-
DF-V1, ACC decreased from 78.06% to 73.28%, AUC from 88.40% to 80.95%, and AP 
from 92.45% to 87.78%; on Celeb-DF-V2, ACC decreased from 75.19% to 64.35%, AUC 
from 79.00% to 74.85%, and AP from 86.58% to 84.55%; on DFDCP, ACC decreased from 
71.43% to 70.26%, AUC from 75.88% to 74.13%, and AP from 83.96% to 82.13%.
When both modules were removed, the performance decline became even more pro­
nounced: on Celeb-DF-V1, ACC dropped to 68.59%, AUC to 74.18%, and AP to 82.44%;

ACC dropped to 63.54%, AUC to 73.65%, and AP to 82.05%.
These results demonstrate that both the SFFI and AAML modules are crucial for the 
SFIAD method, as they complement each other to improve the model’s ability to distinguish 
between real and fake data.

4.4  Data augmentation impact analysis
To investigate the impact of data augmentation on model performance, we compared the 
performance of the SFIAD model without data augmentation (SFIAD - Data aug) and the 
SFIAD model with data augmentation on the Celeb-DF-V1, Celeb-DF-V2, and DFDCP 
datasets. As shown in Table 8, data augmentation significantly improved the model’s perfor­
mance: on Celeb-DF-V1, the AUC increased from 80.15% to 88.40%; on Celeb-DF-V2, the 
AUC increased from 72.96% to 79.00%; and on DFDCP, the AUC increased from 73.40% 
to 75.88%. These experimental results validate the effectiveness of the data augmentation 
strategy.
5  Conclusion
This study proposes a frame-level deepfake detection framework that combines Spatial-
Frequency Feature Integration (SFFI) and Authenticity-Aware Margin Loss (AAML), sig­
nificantly improving the accuracy and robustness of fake content detection. Our method 
effectively integrates spatial and frequency domain features of images, enabling the model 
to more effectively capture subtle differences in forged images. AAML alleviates the class 
imbalance issue by dynamically adjusting the margins, particularly improving classification 
performance in cases where authentic samples are scarce. Experimental results on multiple 
challenging datasets demonstrate that our method outperforms eight existing mainstream 
detection methods in terms of accuracy, AUC, and AP, confirming its strong potential in 
deepfake detection. In the future, we plan to extend this framework to video-level deepfake 
detection and further incorporate spatiotemporal inconsistencies to enhance the model’s 
generalization ability.
