Abstract—The 
proliferation 
of 
deepfake 
technology, 
powered by advanced generative models such as generative 
adversarial networks (GANs), presents challenges in digital 
media authenticity, public trust, and cybersecurity. We 
reviewed recent advancements in deepfake detection across 
multiple modalities, including image, video, and audio. 
Benchmark datasets, such as FaceForensics++, the deepfake 
detection challenge (DFDC), and Celeb-deepfake (Celeb-DF), 
have been used to develop diverse detection models. These 
models encompass approaches based on EfficientNet-driven 
transfer learning, convolutional neural network–long short-
term memory (CNN-LSTM) hybrids for temporal feature 
extraction, graph-based neural architectures, and ensemble 
methods that integrate deep learning with handcrafted features. 
Although certain models report detection accuracies as high as 
99.99% 
on 
specific 
datasets, 
many 
exhibit 
limited 
generalizability across different benchmarks, particularly when 
confronted with compression artifacts. Additionally, real-time 
deployment remains constrained by substantial computational 
demands. 
Emerging 
threats, 
including 
adversarial 
perturbations and diffusion-based synthetic media, necessitate 
the development of more resilient detection strategies. Proactive 
countermeasures such as blockchain-based timestamping, 
digital watermarking, and cryptographic hashing have been 
adopted to enhance media integrity. The results of the review 
underscore the need for lightweight, interpretable, and 
multimodal detection frameworks to generalize the models’ 
applicability across diverse domains, thereby supporting 
reliable and scalable media verification in increasingly complex 
digital environments.ion. 
Keywords—deepfake detection, generative adversarial networks

I. INTRODUCTION 
Deepfakes are the synthetic media created using deep 
learning models, particularly generative adversarial networks 
(GANs), where visual or audio content is manipulated to 
appear convincingly authentic. These technologies generate 
highly realistic fake videos in which individuals appear to say 
or do things they never do in reality, posing a growing threat 
to digital authenticity and public trust. These realistic 
manipulations of facial expressions, voices, and body 
movements 
have 
been 
exploited 
in 
identity 
theft, 
misinformation, and political propaganda [1,2]. As deepfakes 
become increasingly sophisticated and accessible, their 
potential for misuse expands, raising serious ethical, legal, and 
societal concerns. 
The proliferation of deepfake technology raises ethical and 
legal 
concerns, 
particularly 
around 
impersonation, 
misinformation, and privacy infringement. The unauthorized 
creation of synthetic media, especially explicit or defamatory 
content, poses threats to individuals and institutions alike. In 
response, legislative efforts, such as the Deepfakes 
Accountability Act in the United States, criminalize malicious 
usage, yet enforcement remains challenging in the absence of 
admissible and standardized detection tools. 
The psychological effect on victims is significant, ranging 
from anxiety to reputational damage and job loss, highlighting 
the need for greater institutional responsibility and increased 
public awareness. Platforms that host user-generated content 
must enforce strict policies, promote media literacy, and 
implement reliable detection mechanisms. Additionally, 
ethical AI deployment should involve transparent model 
behavior, fair dataset collection practices, and explainable 
outputs to help prevent misuse and ensure accountability. 
Researchers have developed a wide array of detection 
methods and benchmark datasets to mitigate these threats [3]. 
However, performance often varies significantly due to 
differences in dataset characteristics, such as in healthcare [4], 
manipulation types, and media quality. These variations 
underscore the need for detection models that are robust, 
scalable, and explainable across diverse scenarios. 
The application of deepfake detection systems is 
expanding across various industries [5]. In journalism, real-
time detection tools help authenticate breaking news and 
identify disinformation campaigns [6,7]. Social media 
platforms, including Facebook and YouTube, are integrating 
artificial intelligence-based moderation systems to flag 
potentially manipulated content, aiding in content integrity 
and user safety.

In the financial sector, facial recognition systems are 
increasingly targeted by deepfake attacks, prompting the 
adoption of advanced liveness detection, multi-angle 
validation, and scene consistency checks. A notable case study 
involves telemedicine [8] and remote healthcare consultation, 
where verifying the authenticity of video feeds is crucial. 
Ensuring that a patient’s identity and appearance are unaltered 
enhances diagnostic reliability and ensures compliance with 
healthcare regulations. 
In this study, we conducted a structured review of current 
deepfake detection methods, organized by architectural 
approach, dataset usage, and performance. We explored the 
evolution from convolutional neural network (CNN)-based 
systems to advanced hybrid, transformer, and graph-based 
models, while also examining their limitations, including 
issues of cross-dataset generalizability and real-time 
feasibility. In addition, we explored emerging prevention 
techniques and future trends to enhance media integrity and 
trust. 
II. METHODOLOGY  
We evaluated peer-reviewed IEEE-indexed articles 
published between 2020 and 2025, related to deepfake 
detection strategies. Each study was assessed using a 
standardized rubric, considering model architecture, datasets 
used, detection approach, performance metrics, and strengths 
and limitations. Through the assessment, emerging trends 
such as transformer-based models, multimodal fusion, and 
adversarial robustness challenges were identified [3,6,18]. 
III. DEEPFAKE DETECTION APPROACHES 
In recent years, the rapid increase of deepfake content has 
sparked significant interest in developing robust detection 
methods [14]. Deepfake detection methods distinguish 
between real and synthetically generated media using a 
variety of computational techniques. These methods are 
categorized into traditional machine learning, deep learning 
[15], and hybrid approaches. While early deepfake detection 
methods were developed based on traditional machine 
learning algorithms that relied on manually extracted facial 
or motion features, recent methods have adopted deep 
learning approaches capable of automatic feature extraction 
and representation learning. 
A. CNNs 
CNNs are the backbone of many deepfake detection 
systems due to their ability to extract spatial features from 
images and videos. XceptionNet, EfficientNet-B7, and 
custom CNN models have demonstrated high accuracy on 
benchmark datasets such as FaceForensics++ and Celeb-DF.

multi-task cascaded convolutional networks (MTCNN) have 
achieved up to 97.70% accuracy on the FaceForensics++ 
benchmark [9]. Similarly, hybrid architectures such as 
VGG16–CNN have demonstrated 95% accuracy on a Kaggle 
dataset comprising real and fake facial images, highlighting 
the efficacy of transfer learning and hierarchical feature 
extraction [7].  
Despite these impressive benchmark results, CNN-based 
deepfake detection models have limitations in practical 
deployment. High computational overhead, including 
substantial storage and memory requirements, impedes real-
time performance. Moreover, these models exhibit poor 
generalization when applied to unseen datasets or more 
sophisticated forgery techniques, resulting in notable 
performance degradation (Table I) [6,11].

B. Spatial-Temporal and Hybrid Models

researchers have integrated temporal modeling with spatial 
feature 
extraction. 
Hybrid 
models, 
including 
InceptionResNetV2 
+ 
LSTM , 
analyze 
frame-level 
inconsistencies in videos, achieving 91.48% accuracy on Face 
Forensics++ in 40 epochs [12]. Another notable example

as Deepfake Detection Challenge (DFDC) and Celeb-

capturing dynamic artifacts such as unnatural facial 
movement, mismatched lip-sync, or abnormal eye movement 
are the key indicators of manipulation, revealing subtle flaws 
introduced during deepfake generation. However, these 
models are now easily deployed due to high computational

C. Graph-Based and Transformer Approaches 
Recent 
advancements 
include 
graph-based 
and 
transformer architectures, which offer superior generalization 
and interpretability. GatedGCN, paired with EfficientNet-b0, 
models region-wise facial inconsistencies (e.g., eyes, nose, 
mouth) as graph nodes, achieving an area under the curve 
(AUS) of 98.7% on FMFCC-V and robust cross-dataset 
performance (a 89.4% AUC on Celeb-DF) [16]. Transformer-
based methods such as Cross-ViT + DenseNet leverage multi-
scale attention to fuse global and local features, attaining state-
of-the-art results (an AUC of 99.99% on DeeperForensics 1.0) 
[17]. Other models, such as spatial kernel selection and halo 
attention network (SKSHA-Net), dynamically adjust spatial 
receptive fields to detect subtle artifacts, achieving a 99.32% 
AUC on FaceForensics++ HQ [18]. Despite their success, 
these models are computationally intensive and may struggle 
with low-quality or compressed media [19,20].

IV. DATASET AND PERFORMANCE  
A thorough understanding of the datasets used in deepfake 
detection research is crucial for evaluating the effectiveness 
and generalizability of proposed models. The performance of 
detection algorithms varies significantly depending on the 
dataset used for training and testing, as each dataset contains 
different types of manipulations, quality levels, and facial 
variations [20]. Moreover, while several models perform well 
within a single dataset, they might struggle to maintain 
accuracy across different datasets or under challenging 
conditions. We analyzed commonly used deepfake datasets 
and evaluated intra-dataset performance to explore cross-
dataset generalization issues and the robustness of detection 
methods in adversarial scenarios. 
A. Common Datasets and Intra-Dataset Performance 
The most widely used datasets include FaceForensics++, 
DFDC, Celeb-DF, and DeeperForensics 1.0, which provide 
diverse samples of manipulated images and videos. Models 
trained and tested on the same dataset often achieve high 
performance. For instance, SFSimNet reached a 99.18% AUC 
on FaceForensics++ (HQ) [21] while AMTENnet achieved a 
98.52% accuracy on the same dataset [22]. However, intra-
dataset results can be misleading, as models overfit to specific 
artifacts or manipulation techniques [3,23]. 
B. Common Cross-Dataset Generalization Challenges 
A significant limitation in deepfake detection is a decrease 
in performance when models are tested on unseen datasets. 
For 
example, frequency-enhanced 
self-blended 
images 
(FSBI), which combine SBI with discrete wavelet transform 
(DWT), achieved a 95.49% AUC on Celeb-DF but struggled 
with NeuralTextures due to unique artifact patterns [18]. 
Similarly, the temporal transformer network (TTNet), which 
leverages facial landmarks for detection, showed an average 
cross-dataset AUC of 83.6%, highlighting the difficulty of 
generalizing across diverse manipulation methods [20]. High-
frequency diversified augmentation (HFDA) mitigates this 
issue by perturbing high-frequency components in the Fourier 
domain, improving cross-dataset AUC to 85.09% on Celeb-
DF-v2 [24]. 
C. Robustness to Adversarial Conditions 
Deepfake detectors must also contend with adversarial 
attacks, compression artifacts, and low-resolution inputs. A 
multi-scale graph attention transformer (MSG-GAT), a 
spectral-guided graph attention network, demonstrated 
resilience to adversarial conditions, achieving a 99.3% 
accuracy on Celeb-DF [25]. Conversely, vision transformer 
(ViT) 
+ 
EfficientNet models 
exhibited 
performance 
degradation on low-quality videos, underscoring the need for 
robust preprocessing and adaptive architectures [26]. The lack 
of standardized evaluation protocols further complicates 
comparisons, as AUC and accuracy vary based on dataset 
splits and manipulation types [23,27]. 
V. STANDARDIZATION AND BENCHMARKING  
While numerous datasets and evaluation metrics exist, the 
lack of universal benchmarking standards hampers model 
comparability and reproducibility. Discrepancies in video 
resolution, compression artifacts, manipulation techniques, 
and data partitioning lead to inconsistent and misleading 
performance metrics. For instance, a model trained on high-
quality synthetic videos fail on real-world compressed videos 
sourced from social media. 
To address this, the unified evaluation protocols and a 
centralized leaderboard are required to assess detection 
models across diverse datasets. Incorporating advanced 
metrics such as compression robustness, adversarial resistance, 
demographic fairness, and real-time latency enables an 
understanding of system performance. These benchmarks 
improve research rigor to build confidence among 
stakeholders adopting detection technologies. 
VI. PREVENTION OF DEEPFAKE 
Several studies have proposed prevention mechanisms to 
authenticate media and counteract the malicious use of 
deepfakes. Blockchain timestamping [28], watermark 
injection [28], and cryptographic hashing [29] are considered 
in verifying the originality and integrity of media content. 
However, these approaches face limitations in terms of 
scalability, susceptibility to anti-forensic attacks, and the need 
for cross-platform adoption. As a result, their effectiveness 
remains constrained in real-world settings. 
Emerging trends indicate a shift toward proactive and 
intelligent detection systems that integrate explainable 
artificial intelligence (XAI), federated learning, and real-time 
deployment 
optimization. 
Models 
leveraging 
vision 
transformers, GAN discriminators, and multimodal biometric 
analysis are gaining prominence due to their enhanced 
capability to detect and localize manipulated regions with 
greater precision [10,17,22]. These advancements enable the 
development of more resilient and adaptive detection 
frameworks, capable of effectively responding to the rapid 
evolution of deepfake generation techniques. 
VII. CONCLUSION 
Despite remarkable advancements in deepfake detection, 
challenges persist. Current methods, particularly deep 
learning models, often struggle with generalization, high 
computational cost, and real-time deployment feasibility. 
While hybrid and ensemble models demonstrate superior 
accuracy, they are often hindered by increased complexity and 
latency. Moreover, low-quality or adversarial manipulations 
continue to expose vulnerabilities in existing systems. Further 
research is necessary to develop adaptive, lightweight, and 
interpretable detection models supported by standardized 
benchmarks and robust cross-domain evaluation protocols. 
Interdisciplinary 
collaboration 
and 
timely 
regulatory 
measures are essential in ensuring secure and trustworthy AI-
driven media authentication. 
To ensure long-term success in deepfake detection, the 
current 
limitations 
in 
generalizability, 
efficiency, 
explainability, and reproducibility of existing models must be 
addressed. 
First, cross-dataset training and evaluation must be 
prioritized, as generalization across datasets remains one of 
the most pressing challenges in deepfake detection. Models 
trained on specific datasets often fail to adapt to unfamiliar 
manipulation techniques and real-world conditions. Although 
many detection models achieve high accuracy on their training 
datasets, performance degrades when applied to unseen data 
with differing characteristics. To address this limitation, cross-
dataset evaluation must become a standard practice to ensure 
robustness in unpredictable environments. A lack of 
generalizability can be mitigated through domain adaptation

techniques, including adversarial training, style transfer, and 
data augmentation, which enable models to learn invariant 
features across heterogeneous data distributions. Additionally, 
multimodal fusion, integrating visual, audio, and textual cues, 
has demonstrated promise in improving detection accuracy 
across domains [30,31]. 
Second, 
researchers 
must 
develop 
low-latency 
architectures for mobile and edge deployment. Practical 
deepfake detection requires models to operate efficiently on 
resource-constrained 
devices 
such 
as 
smartphones, 
surveillance systems, and embedded platforms. Model 
quantization, pruning, and neural architecture search (NAS) 
can be used to significantly reduce computational complexity 
while preserving performance. Lightweight models that 
maintain high accuracy and enable fast inference are essential 
for real-time detection and scalability. DeepGuardNet, 
specifically designed for edge deployment, exemplifies the 
feasibility of efficient real-time detection [32,33]. 
Third, the integration of XAI is critical. Deep learning 
models are criticized for their lack of interpretability, 
functioning as "black boxes." Explainability modules, such as 
attention heatmaps, feature attribution maps, and saliency 
visualizations, enhance transparency and facilitate an 
understanding of model behavior. This is particularly 
important in legal and forensic contexts, where model 
decisions must be justified in formal proceedings. Techniques 
like Grad-CAM and saliency visualization offer valuable 
insights into decision-making processes [34,35]. 
Fourth, future systems must adopt human-AI collaboration. 
While AI excels at processing large volumes of data, human 
judgment remains indispensable in scenarios requiring 
nuanced interpretation. Hybrid workflows that combine 
automated detection with manual verification yield more 
reliable outcomes, especially in sensitive domains such as 
journalism, legal evidence validation, and psychological 
assessment. Interfaces that support interactive feedback and 
user-in-the-loop mechanisms will enhance trust, usability, and 
adoption. 
Finally, open-source frameworks are required to foster 
reproducibility 
and 
transparency. 
Publicly 
available 
codebases, benchmark datasets, and evaluation results enable 
researchers to validate results, compare methodologies, and 
build upon previous results. The adoption of standardized 
evaluation protocols and shared repositories accelerates 
innovation and reduces redundant efforts. The findable, 
accessible, interoperable, and reusable (FAIR) principles in 
data sharing must be employed to cultivate a collaborative and 
sustainable ecosystem for deepfake research. 
Collectively, these recommendations help to bridge the 
gap between academic innovation and practical deployment, 
ensuring that deepfake detection systems are robust, efficient, 
interpretable, and accessible across a wide range of 
applications.
