ABSTRACT 
Recent advancements in deep learning have given rise 
to new forms of media that are extremely realistic yet 
also create major threats to the integrity and trust of the 
digital world—synthetic media, commonly referred to 
as deepfakes. Deepfake audio and video have quickly 
become an easy medium to commit identity fraud, 
produce misinformation campaigns, and conduct 
advanced social engineering attacks.This article 
describes an AI-based multimodal detection system for 
detecting deepfake content in both video and audio 
formats via the use of state-of-the-art deep neural 
network architectures. For video-based detection, 
CNN-based spatial feature extractors (EfficientNet and 
ResNet50) are combined with temporal modeling via 
BiLSTM and an attention mechanism to isolate subtle 
facial artifacts present in deepfake content. For the 
detection of deepfake audio, the proposed framework 
incorporates several state-of-the-art anti-spoofing 
models (RawNet for raw waveform learning, LCNN 
for artifact-based spectral detection, AASIST for 
graph-attention fusion, and CNNs trained on MFCC 
and spectrogram features) to identify manipulation 
artifacts of various types within the synthesized 
speech.The proposed solution executes on a secure 
full-stack web application with visual analytics, 
confidence scores, and automated forensic PDF writing 
functionality. Evaluation on standard benchmark 
datasets shows that the proposed architecture achieves 
high accuracy levels and near real-time inference, 
demonstrating 
the 
systems’ 
value 
for 
digital 
forensics/cybersecurity. 
 
 
I. INTRODUCTION 
The rapid rise of generative AI has made the creation 
of 
synthetic 
media—both 
video 
and 
audio—
astonishingly simple and highly convincing. Modern 
deepfake systems can replicate facial movements, 
vocal tones, speech patterns, and subtle human 
behaviours 
with 
near-human 
accuracy. 
While 
deepfakes have beneficial applications in film 
production, assistive technologies, and education, their 
misuse has grown far more rapidly. Attackers now 
exploit deepfakes for identity theft, impersonation 
scams, misinformation campaigns, and manipulation 
of digital trust. 
Traditional authenticity-verification methods, such as 
digital watermarks or metadata tracing, are no longer 
reliable because these indicators can be stripped, 
modified, or completely bypassed. As deepfake 
generation advances—particularly with diffusion 
models and high-capacity GAN architectures—the 
visual cues like unnatural blinking, head tilts, or 
mismatched lighting are far less detectable. This shift 
has pushed researchers toward deep learning–based 
detection, especially CNNs and hybrid CNN–RNN 
systems that can learn fine-grained spatial and temporal 
inconsistencies. 
In parallel, audio deepfakes have evolved dramatically. 
With just a few seconds of audio, modern voice-
cloning systems can imitate a person’s voice well 
enough to deceive humans and automated verification 
systems. This has led to real-world attacks, including 
fraudulent CEO-voice scams, bypassing voice-based 
authentication, and manipulating customer-support 
channels. To counter this, researchers now rely on 
advanced audio-processing architectures such as 
RawNet for raw-waveform learning, LCNN for

                            II. Literature Review 
Deepfake detection has become a multidisciplinary 
subject that brings together people from many different 
disciplines such as computer vision, audio and speech 
research, cybersecurity, and digital forensics. All of 
these people are responding to an evolving threat and 
the need to detect deepfakes. 
 
In this section, we will review the main categories of 
detection approaches that have been developed in 
recent years. We will also discuss how detection 
techniques are currently failing. What we can say is 
that there is no silver bullet solution to this problem yet. 
Additionally, with the recent improvements in the 
quality of deepfake generator software, many of the 
tools that have been developed over the last several 
years to detect deepfakes are not as effective. 
 
A.Traditional Forensic Approaches 
 
In the early days of multimedia forensics, the majority 
of evidence used to determine if an image or video was 
faked relied on identifying the unusual features caused 
by various aspects of the recording process. For 
example, researchers would analyze the color filter 
arrays on the cameras that took the picture, look for 
compression artifacts that didn't match other nearby 
frames, and find physical features that were either 
misaligned or inconsistent (such as lighting that didn't 
match across a subject's face). 
 
Many researchers wrote papers on things such as how 
long a person takes to blink at least one time (regularity 
of blinks), unnatural head pose transitions, and odd 
ratios in body parts/geometry, etc. as being examples 
of potential signs of manipulation. 
 
These methods had the advantage of being quick to run, 
having relatively small datasets, and providing a 
reasonable explanation for their findings. However, 
these methods are now outdated. Today's ML-based 
generators (especially diffusion-based ones that have 
become popular recently) were developed specifically 
to minimize many of the types of anomalies 
characterized by these older methods. As a result, many 
of the signs that would have once been reliable 
indicators of alteration are now unlikely to be valid 
indicators, as many high-quality deepfakes pass 
through current validation methods with little or no 
signs left behind. 
 
B. CNN-Based Visual Detection 
 
The development of deep CNNs has dramatically 
improved detection. Examples of organizations using 
deep CNNs to detect forgeries include XceptionNet, 
ResNet, EfficientNet, and a variety of their derivatives. 
These models can learn to detect spatial patterns from 
images of the face, including blends of edges, odd 
frequencies, and anomalies in texture, that humans 
cannot easily detect. There are large public databases, 
such as FaceForensics++ (as well as extensions), that 
provide researchers and developers with many labelled 
forgeries that were created with many different 
methods , which helps to improve the quality of model 
training. 
 
One significant problem with deep CNNs is that they 
are still primarily based on analysing images frame-by 
frame, and attempt to treat each frame with almost no 
relation to other frames. As a result, many types of 
motion and temporal variations that are present in 
video do not display their full effects when using deep 
CNNs. E.g. A forgeries that is absolutely photorealistic 
with any temporal inconsistency in the flow of facial 
expression and/or movement of the mouth will be 
indistinguishable from a real one when viewed in 
isolation, however, when viewed in motion the 
inconsistencies in flow will become significant enough 
to be detected by a human observer. The performance 
of many models that detect newer (or are created more 
like older) forgeries when tested in real-world 
conditions 
will 
significantly 
differ 
from 
the 
performance levels that they achieve when tested in 
controlled lab conditions.. 
 
C. Temporal and Hybrid CNN–RNN Models 
 
In order to work around the absence of a time 
dimension, users began combining recurrent layers 
(LSTMs, GRUs, sometimes BiLSTMs) with CNN 
feature extractors. The logic behind this technique is 
sound: to allow models to learn dependencies across 
frames in order to catch unnatural rates of blinking, 
sudden jerky expressions, mismatches between lips 
and sounds, and motions that don’t appear to flow 
correctly. Based on research conducted by us and by 
others, these combinations appear to perform better on 
video-based fakes (in particular, more subtle fakes) 
than models using only static images.

In terms of video, the process looks at groups of

overall images (i.e. the way that each object appears on 
screen) based upon spatial inconsistencies (for 
example, an object showing up with an unusual surface 
texture) and temporal inconsistencies (i.e. the objects 
movement between frames is jerky, i.e. that the 
character’s facial expression in one frame does not 
coincide with them in a later frame). 
 
In terms of audio, we look at factors related to speech 
characteristics such as prosodic characteristics (the 
rhythm of speech), the characteristics of vowel sounds 
as they occur relative to other sounds that occur in same 
time period (called formants), and variations in 
breathing sounds or other distinctive characteristics 
that can be detected with respect to synthetic vs non-
synthetic. 
 
The same overall detection pipeline is used for both 
video and audio, making it easier to implement and 
manage. 
 
B. Threat Model 
We work under the attack model of an adversary that 
has access to the best generative tools available today, 
such as advanced GANs, diffusion models, and neural 
voice cloning pipelines, and is capable of producing 
highly-realistic forgeries. 
The types of manipulations that we are considering 
(and not limited to) include: 
 
➢ face swapping and facial reenactment techniques 
➢ voice cloning combined with neural text-to-speech 
synthesis 
➢ artificial lip-sync adjustments to match dubbed or 
fabricated audio 
➢ cross-modal impersonations, where visual and 
auditory elements are swapped or blended to 
impersonate a target person 
 
The primary goal of the attacker will be to produce 
content so convincing that it will fool both casual 
consumers and automated systems such as biometric 
verifiers, content moderation platforms, and forensic 
authentication systems. As we are making worst-case 
assumptions with this work, our detector must be able 
to detect forgeries that achieve very high perceptual 
fidelity with respect to both sight and sound. 
 
C. Design Objectives 
 
Our deepfake detection framework is based on 
identifying four top priorities: that the framework is 
highly temporally aware, that it provides effective 
discriminative feature extraction, that it identifies 
realistic deployment constraints, and that it has real 
utility in a Cybersecurity context. 
 
In terms of temporal sensitivity, we have put the 
highest 
emphasis 
on 
detecting 
motion-based 
manipulation. Many signs of tampering are only 
evident with the movement of objects over time. For 
example, a person's abnormal blink rate (or unusual 
rate of blinking during the recording), their inconsistent 
micro-expression movements or odd head tilt 
movements over time, and an audio-visual de-
synchronisation will be extremely difficult for a user to 
spot through static or single frame basis assessments of 
a video. Temporal motion sensitivity will enable the 
model to identify the sequential relationships within a 
video, which ultimately will give the model a better 
chance of identifying the covert and overt indicators of 
tampering.

With regard to highly discriminative features, we focus 
on constructing highly discriminative representations 
to represent the features of an object by employing 
deep neural networks. Modern deep networks 
automatically identify spatial features of an object 
(e.g., faces, backgrounds, illumination) and acoustic 
features (e.g., spectral patterns, pitch contours) that 
have demonstrated to provide specific and sensitive 
indicators of tampering on an Object. In addition to 
these features, attention layer features will assist in the 
identification and evaluation of the most suspicious or 
malign parts of the object and will be very robust in 
defining the object when inputs are under distortion 
caused by compression, noise, or low resolution, as 
would occur in most real-life situations. 
 
With regard to practicality, it is extremely important to 
us that we use modular components that are efficient in 
use of CPU resources and that operate on a commodity-
standard graphics processing unit (GPU), and that 
provide real-time or nearly real-time processing 
capabilities. By utilising these modular and efficient 
CPU resource economies, we can readily integrate a 
deepfake detection capable framework into live 
monitoring setups, as well as into mobile applications 
and forensic workstations without requiring overly 
burdensome hardware. 
 
IV. Deep Learning Methodology 
 
 
 
 
A.Video Preprocessing 
 
Individual video clips are decomposed into single 
frames; this is done via extraction at a rate consistent 
across all videos (e.g., 1-4 frames per second), which 
will vary based on the specifics of each dataset and the 
overall length of the video. To facilitate computational 
efficiency, we use MTCNN, a widely-used multi-task 
cascaded convolutional neural network, to jointly 
detect faces, localize facial landmarks, and align faces. 
The alignment process will crop out the majority of the 
background noise, as well as normalizing the spatial 
locations of the facial parts so they can all be aligned 
consistently to the same orientation and scale, across 
all video frames. 
 
Finally, we resize and normalize all of the previously 
extracted facial patches to a predetermined size (e.g., 
224 x 224 pixels or 256 x 256 pixels) using the 
mean/standard deviation statistics from a pre-trained 
model of a backbone network, or if no such model can 
be found, the appropriate mean/standard deviation 
statistics from the overall dataset. 
 
In order to capture temporal information while staying 
within the limits of memory/compute capability, we 
uniformly sample a small number of frames (e.g., 16-
64, depending on the length of the video) across the 
entire video, providing an effective compromise 
between short and long term dynamics while 
eliminating redundancy associated with dense frame 
extraction. 
 
 
B. Spatial Feature Extraction via CNN Backbone 
 
For frame-based visual analysis, we commonly utilize 
EfficientNet-B0 or statistically, ResNet-50, due to their 
lightweight design while maintaining a high level of 
performance. Both networks are initialized with 
ImageNet-pretrained weights; therefore, both networks 
effectively extract semantically rich features that reveal 
visual manipulation artifacts including, but not limited 
to: visible blending seams, texture mismatches, 
lighting mismatches, unnatural edges where two items 
meet, and subtle facial irregularities/ distortions that 
are frequently missed by the casual observer. 
 
Once the final convolutional layer has been completed, 
we apply global average pooling followed by a small 
flatten to yield a small compact fixed dimensional 
embedding vector per frame (typically 1280-d for 
EfficientNet-B0). These embedding vectors are then 
used as the basis for the temporal aggregation stage that 
follows.

VI. Conclusion and Future Work 
In our paper, we have shared our journey of secretly 
developing a new deepfake detector. More importantly, 
we have demonstrated a couple of cool features such as 
the unified audio, video component and the real, world, 
friendliness without making deepfake detectors the 
only focus. We implemented a combination of 
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to extract 
spatial cues, recurrent layers (BiLSTM) to capture 
temporal dependencies, and attention mechanisms to 
concentrate on the most telling frames, which greatly 
improves the performance of the model compared to 
the methods only using facial frames. Moreover, the 
full, stack implementation with secure handling, 
detailed logging, and automatic forensic reports 
converts a mere laboratory prototype into a feasible 
solution that can be integrated into cybersecurity 
pipelines, forensic laboratories, or media verification 
workflows. 
The experiments pretty much confirmed this. On well, 
known datasets such as FaceForensics++, Celeb, DF 
and DFDC, our method consistently improved 
detection performance and, most importantly, yielded 
greater resilience to various degradations that are 
typical of the real, world conditions: severe 
compression, noise, and low resolution. Such 
sturdiness plus the multimodality feature altogether 
evoke the notion of a significant breakthrough in the 
arsenal of combating the attacks where the adversaries 
are hard to spot because of their trickery in the visuals 
as well as in the auditory fakes. 
However, it really isn't the story's end. In fact, several 
avenues of the project are worth exploring. 
Replacing the current transformer blocks with lighter 
versions (or even hybrid recurrent, transformer blocks) 
might be a way to increase the length of dependencies 
without drastically increasing the inference time. 
Ensuring that the audio and video tell compatible 
stories thus cross, modal consistency seems like a ripe 
avenue for detecting even more sophisticated attacks. 
 
Perhaps, adversarial training would almost certainly be 
a weapon against evasion attempts, and adding better 
explainability (beyond just attention maps) would 
make outputs easier for non, experts to trust and audit. 
 
More generally, we want to delve into federated 
learning configurations to train on decentralized, 
privacy, preserving data sources, to continue to 
overcoming dataset biases through more diverse 
collection and augmentation, and to figure out the 
whole pipeline to work for an edge device or large, 
scale cloud deployment. 
The big picture here is the creation of non, static 
defenses, systems that are able to adjust when new 
generative models (diffusion, based, multimodal large 
models, etc.) keep being released. It is definitely a race 
for superiority, but with smart engineering and 
maintaining the collaboration across the different 
fields, we can be the ones leading instead of always 
being one step behind.
