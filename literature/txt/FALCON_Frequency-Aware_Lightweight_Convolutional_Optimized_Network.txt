Abstract—The rise of deepfakes—images or videos that are 
manipulated using AI techniques like GANs—has created serious 
challenges in the areas of media trust, security, and online safety. 
Many existing methods for detecting deepfakes depend on visible 
image features or large, complex models like transformers. These 
models often struggle to detect newer types of deepfakes and 
require high computational power, which makes them hard to 
use on mobile or low-power devices. To address these issues, we 
propose FALCON (Frequency-Aware Lightweight Convolutional 
Optimized Network), a simple and efficient deep learning model 
for deepfake detection. The main goal of FALCON is to improve 
accuracy while keeping the model small and fast enough to run 
on real-time systems and low-resource devices. FALCON works 
in the frequency domain, where deepfakes often leave hidden 
traces. It uses three key techniques: High-Frequency Residual 
Injection (HFRI) to boost fake signals early in the network, 
Frequency Convolutional Layers (FCL) to analyze amplitude and 
phase patterns, and High-Frequency Refined Features (HFRF) 
to improve feature detection in deeper layers. We trained the 
model using techniques like mixed-precision, gradient clipping, 
and early stopping for better performance and stability. On the 
Kaggle “Deepfake and Real Images” dataset, FALCON achieved 
90% accuracy, 0.90 F1-score, and 0.97 AUC, outperforming 
other lightweight models. FALCON is ideal for real-time, reliable 
deepfake detection on everyday devices. This study highlights the 
effectiveness of frequency-domain learning as a practical and 
generalizable approach for deepfake detection.

II. RELATED WORK 
In this section, recent advancements in deepfake detec- 
tion are reviewed across three categories: foundational meth- 
ods, frequency-domain techniques, and advanced architectures. 
Each group highlights key approaches aimed at enhancing 
detection accuracy, generalization, and robustness. 
A. Foundations and Classical Architectures 
Early deepfake detection techniques explored the limita- 
tions of traditional methods, stressing the need for adaptive 
learning systems to address increasing visual realism [1]. 
Deep learning significantly outperformed rule-based systems, 
offering stronger generalization to facial manipulation patterns 
[2]. Machine learning models trained on face-warping artifacts 
provided early accuracy boosts but lacked robustness across 
domains [3]. Advanced CNNs like NASNetLarge achieved 
high accuracy even on limited data by leveraging automated 
architecture search [4]. Hybrid models combining CNN and 
LSTM architectures captured both spatial and temporal fea- 
tures for video forgery detection [5], which were further 
enhanced by Recycle-GAN modules that adapted to frame- 
level variations [6]. For low-resource settings, lightweight 
CNNs operating in the frequency domain provided efficient 
solutions while maintaining competitive detection rates [7]. 
B. Frequency-Domain and Signal-Based Methods 
Frequency-domain techniques revealed subtle GAN arti- 
facts invisible in the spatial domain. FrePGAN introduced 
perturbations in the spectral space, improving generalization 
across generative models [8]. FreqNet used amplitude and 
phase-aware convolutions to extract high-frequency details 
effectively [9]. DCT-based methods exposed upsampling ar- 
tifacts from synthetic media [10], while self-blended wavelet- 
based models emphasized fine, imperceptible manipulation 
features [11]. Universal detectors employed frequency mask- 
ing to improve robustness across unseen generation methods 
[12]. Graph-based models combined attention and frequency 
learning to detect complex forgeries [13], and distillation tech- 
niques allowed compressed video detection without sacrificing 
accuracy [14]. Bilateral filtering of high-frequency regions was 
also found to be effective across object categories and image 
manipulations [15]. Transformer-based multimodal networks 
further enhanced performance by merging RGB and frequency 
cues [16], while signal-based detection using ENF offered real- 
time, model-free alternatives for verifying media integrity [17], 
[18]. 
C. Advanced Architectures and Real-World Robustness 
Generalization remained a focus, with models leveraging 
high-frequency features to remain effective across datasets 
and forgery types [19]. Two-stream CNNs tackled coarse 
and fine manipulation signals simultaneously [20], and dual- 
domain attention mechanisms improved both classification 
and localization [21]. Detection models trained for real-world 
robustness addressed distribution shifts, compressions, and 
lighting variations [22]. Multi-task learning architectures si- 
multaneously handled classification and manipulation region 
detection, boosting accuracy [23]. DCT-based transformers 
offered frequency-focused attention without relying on con- 
volutions [24]. Finally, combining diffusion reconstruction 
error with frequency features improved localization of subtle 
manipulations, particularly in scientific and academic media 
[25].

E. Network Architecture 
The architecture of FALCON, as shown in Figure 1 , is 
constructed with frequency-aware layers from the input to the 
classifier. It consists of: 
- Entry Block: A shallow convolutional stack followed by 
HFRI to encode frequency-enhanced inputs. 
- Feature Blocks: A sequence of modules including HFRF, 
FCL, and Residual Blocks to learn multi-scale representations. 
- Global Pooling: Adaptive average pooling to compress 
features across spatial dimensions. 
- Classifier: A fully connected layer that converts the pooled 
features into a scalar output. 
Residual connections throughout the architecture help main- 
tain low-level information and stabilize gradient flow, dropout 
layers with a probability of 0.3 were optionally inserted 
after frequency convolutional and residual blocks to reduce 
overfitting and improve generalization, while adaptive pooling 
ensures robustness to spatial variations.

A. Datasets 
We used the publicly available Kaggle dataset, ”Deepfake 
and Real Images,” which includes both real and manipulated 
face images. These images vary in terms of their sources and 
synthesis methods, such as GAN-generated content and face- 
swapping techniques. The dataset was divided into three sub- 
sets: 80% for training, 10% for validation, and 10% for testing. 
Images were resized to 64×64 resolution and normalized using 
ImageNet mean and standard deviation. Basic augmentation, 
including horizontal flipping, was applied during training, 
following practices in prior work such as [9], [14]. 
B. Implementation Details 
FALCON was implemented using PyTorch.Before describ- 
ing the implementation, it is important to note that FALCON’s 
design stands out due to, it’s novel and efficient approach to 
deepfake detection by uniquely combining frequency-domain 
processing with a lightweight convolutional framework. Unlike 
existing models that rely heavily on spatial artifacts or large 
transformer-based architectures, FALCON enhances general- 
ization through high-frequency residual injection (HFRI) and 
dual-domain refinement (HFRF) in both spatial and channel 
dimensions. The addition of Frequency Convolutional Layers 
(FCL) that separately process amplitude and phase allows 
the model to capture subtle GAN-induced artifacts often 
overlooked in traditional approaches. Its low computational 
cost, mixed-precision compatibility, and robust performance 
make it not only state-of-the-art but also suitable for real-time 
deployment on edge devices — a rare combination in current 
deepfake detection research. The training process lasted for 30 
epochs with a batch size of 64, a learning rate of 2 × 10−4, 
and a weight decay of 10−5, utilizing the AdamW optimizer. 
We employed binary cross-entropy loss for training, with 
early stopping applied if no improvement was observed in 
the validation set after 10 epochs. Mixed-precision training 
and gradient clipping (  ∇θ  2 ≤ 1) were used to optimize

Our model leverages frequency-domain information to en- 
hance manipulation detection. The HFRI module extracts high- 
frequency components from images using FFT, masked by 
a high-pass filter with a scale of 8. These filtered signals 
are injected into the model’s early layers. The HFRF module 
operates on intermediate feature maps using FFT along either 
the spatial or channel dimensions, refining their frequency 
representation. 
FCL processes the magnitude and phase of Fourier- 
transformed features separately via depthwise separable con- 
volutions. These are then recombined using the polar form 
and inverse FFT. Residual blocks and adaptive average pooling 
are used for deep representation learning, followed by a final 
linear classifier. This architecture supports generalization by 
promoting spectral awareness, as emphasized in [9], [12], 
[15], and is efficient enough to be deployed in real-time 
environments, contrasting heavier transformer-based solutions 
like [16], [24], and [25]. 
C. Computational Complexity and Deployment Suitability 
One of the main goals of FALCON is to provide an effective 
deepfake detection solution that can operate efficiently on real- 
time and low-resource systems. Unlike many existing models 
that rely on heavy transformer architectures or large-scale 
convolutional backbones, FALCON is designed with compu- 
tational efficiency in mind. The architecture uses depthwise 
separable convolutions in its Frequency Convolutional Layers 
(FCL), significantly reducing the number of parameters and 
floating-point operations (FLOPs) compared to standard con- 
volutions. The input images are resized to 64×64 resolution, 
further lowering the computational burden while retaining 
sufficient detail for deepfake detection. FALCON incorporates 
techniques like mixed-precision training and gradient clip- 
ping, which reduce memory usage and training time without 
sacrificing model performance. During training, the use of 
early stopping prevents overfitting and avoids unnecessary 
computation by halting when validation performance stops 
improving.

E. Comparison with Existing Methods 
We compared FALCON with various different methods 
from recent literature. As shown in Table II, it delivers com- 
petitive results while maintaining computational simplicity. 
Compared to BiHPF [15] and Dual-Domain Attention [21], 
our model achieves similar F1-score and AUC, outperforming 
lightweight CNNs [7] in both robustness and precision. 
F. Effectiveness Evaluation 
To validate the effectiveness of FALCON, we conducted 
additional experiments focusing on generalization, robustness, 
and efficiency. When tested on unseen datasets like CelebDF 
and FaceForensics++, FALCON maintained high F1-scores 
(0.88 and 0.89), confirming strong generalization. The model 
also showed robustness under real-world degradations such as 
JPEG compression, resizing, and Gaussian noise, with only a 
minor drop in performance. In terms of efficiency, FALCON 
contains just 1.3 million parameters and runs at 48 images 
per second on a mobile-class GPU, making it suitable for 
real-time applications. An ablation study further demonstrated 
that removing any frequency-domain module (HFRI, HFRF, or 
FCL) led to noticeable drops in performance, highlighting their 
importance. These results collectively confirm that FALCON 
is not only accurate but also generalizable, robust, lightweight, 
and effective for deployment in practical deepfake detection 
scenarios.

V. CONCLUSION 
In this work, we introduced FALCON, a frequency-aware 
lightweight approach for deepfake detection that efficiently 
and accurately differentiates between real and fake images. 
Tested on the Kaggle ”Deepfake and Real Images” dataset, 
FALCON achieved 90.11% accuracy, an F1 score of 89.63%, 
and an AUC of 96.98%. The classification report highlights 
its effectiveness, with balanced precision and recall across 
classes. The confusion matrix and precision-recall curves 
further confirm reliability, showing few false positives and 
negatives. Compared to existing frequency-domain methods 
like BiHPF, FALCON demonstrates superior accuracy and 
robustness. 
However, some practical limitations remain. FALCON has 
been validated mainly on images, and its performance on 
video-based deepfakes is yet to be explored. While robust 
to compression and resizing, extreme distortions or novel 
manipulations may affect detection. Additionally, further op- 
timization is needed for ultra low-power devices and high- 
resolution real-time processing. 
In future work, we aim to enhance generalization across di- 
verse datasets, optimize computational efficiency, and explore 
multi-modal and video-based detection. Extending FALCON 
with multimodal fusion and multi-task learning also offers 
promising directions for developing more reliable and adapt- 
able forgery detection systems.
