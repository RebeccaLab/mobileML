Abstract—With the emergence of Deepfake technology and
AI-powered media, some digital threats are now being intro-
duced to digital authenticity and trustworthiness. Unlike the
traditional deepfake detection that solely focused on accuracy
with the use of expensive GPU infrastructure, we develop a
practical, CPU-optimized real-time detection system for people
with limited computational resources. This paper utilizes a
ResNet18 convolutional neural network that is optimized for
deployment on consumer-grade CPU hardware (Intel Core i5).
In the core model, it includes a Flask-based web application with
features such as user authentication, role-based access control, file
encryption, batch processing, and analysis history tracking. The
architecture appears pretty modular and scalable. This system
achieves 72.37% validation Accuracy on a curated dataset of
2,041 face images, with an inference time of under 1.5 seconds
per image, which demonstrates deepfake detection can be done
effectively without any GPU acceleration. With massive datasets
(e.g., FaceForensics++: 120,000+ images), this architecture is
projected to achieve up to 90% accuracy while maintaining
real-time CPU-based inference. This efforts illustrate practical
deepfake detection technology deployed effectively without high-
configured or specialized hardware infrastructure, ultimately
making the capability available to a wide range of organizations.

I. INTRODUCTION
The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) and
generative adversarial networks (GANs) has made it possible
to create highly realistic fake images and videos, commonly
known as deepfakes. [1]. These synthetic media are becoming
increasingly difficult to distinguish from real content, creating
serious concerns for digital trust, online security, and media
integrity. The existing research has already shown the ease
of manipulating the expressions, identities, and movements of
faces by utilizing the GAN framework [2]. With the spread
of this deepfake technology, it has become a major societal
threat by enabling misinformation, impersonation, social en-
gineering, political manipulation, and evidence tampering [3].
To tackle these growing challenges, new studies have pro-
posed a range of techniques for the detection of deepfakes.
Traditional detection methods have been based on the design
of hand-crafted features. Some of the traditional detection
techniques for deepfakes include the analysis of the dynamics
of the eye blinks of a person in the image, the lack of
proper head movements of the person in the image, and
the analysis of the biological signals in the image
[4], [5].
These techniques have become ineffective due to the rapid
advancement of the image forgery techniques of deepfakes.
Recent detection techniques have been based on the use of
CNN models. For example, the FaceForensics++ challenge
demonstrated the efficiency of the Xception model of the
CNN in the detection of deepfakes when performed on a GPU
platform [6]. Capsule-forensics was later used to boost the
efficiencies of the detection tasks of deepfakes in images [7].
Approaches involving modeling of temporal relationships in
videos for deepfake detection have shown promising results in
LSTMs & R-CNN variants [8]. Additionally, attention variants
have allowed for detailed detection of minute visual anomalies
in images [9], [10].
Despite the achievement of remarkable progress, the latest
research in the area of deepfake detection faces a number
of challenges. Nearly all high-performance deepfake detection
techniques rely on the support of GPUs in both the training
and inference phases. Architectures including Xception [11],
MobileNet, ShuffleNet [12], [13], or transformers in detec-
tors require a high number of computations. This limits the
reality of real-time processing on the architectures in low-
end hardware. Another problem in the existing literature is
the lack of consideration for real-life metrics concerning the
challenges of deepfake detection in the existing literature.
Almost all existing deepfake detection systems fail to sup-
port real-time processing in the case of CPU computation.
Additionally, the existing research relies on the processing of
controlled environments without emphasizing the importance
of the mentioned real-life challenges.
To overcome the challenges mentioned above, the authors
propose a CPU-optimized real-time deep fake detection sys-
tem based on a lightweight ResNet18 architecture. Unlike
the existing studies relying on GPUs for their computation
efficiency, the proposed solution highlights the efficiency of
the inference process on a general CPU. The proposed solution
possesses a number of benefits compared to existing solutions
that rely on the utilization of GPUs. Firstly, the solution
makes the hardware more accessible without the need for
utilizing the expensive resources of the GPUs. Secondly,
the solution provides real-time processing capabilities for the
detection of deep fake images on the CPU. This makes the
solution more efficient for application in real-world scenarios.

II. RELATED WORKS
This section audits the existing literature on real-time deep-
fake detection. We discuss techniques for deepfake generation
for web-based applications, methods for deepfake detection,
key datasets discussion on deepfakes, the role of machine
learning and deep learning in deepfake modeling, and learning
approaches.
Several key studies have significantly shaped the develop-
ment of deepfake detection research. Rossler et al. [6] intro-
duced the FaceForensics++ dataset, a comprehensive bench-
mark incorporating multiple manipulation techniques, and
demonstrated that CNN-based architectures XceptionNet can
surpass 95% accuracy on high-quality datasets. However, their
approach depends heavily on GPU resources, limiting CPU-
based deployment. Nguyen et al. [7] advanced the field through
Capsule-Forensics, which leverages capsule networks to model
spatial hierarchies of manipulated features and achieves up
to 97% accuracy, but suffers from high computational cost
and slow CPU inference. Chollet’s Xception architecture [11],
widely adopted for deepfake detection, achieves state-of-the-
art performance on datasets on DFDC and FaceForensics++
using depthwise separable convolutions, yet its large parameter
size results in substantial latency when executed on CPUs.
Sabir et al. [8] further improved video-based detection using a
hybrid CNN–RNN framework capable of capturing temporal
inconsistencies across frames, although the method requires
GPU acceleration due to its computationally intensive tem-
poral modeling. More recently, Zhao et al. [9] ntroduced
a multi attentional network that detects fine grained facial
artifacts and generalizes well across datasets, though its at-
tention mechanisms require substantial GPU memory and are
impractical for CPU only systems. In summary, these studies
demonstrate the strong accuracy of modern deepfake detectors
while also showing their heavy dependence on GPU hardware,
highlighting the need for lightweight and CPU optimized
alternatives.
Further advancements in lightweight and efficient model
design have been explored through several important con-
tributions. Howard et al. [12] introduced MobileNets, which
use depthwise separable convolutions to achieve competitive
accuracy with lower computational cost, although their ca-
pacity is often too limited for complex deepfake datasets.
Zhang et al. [13] extended this work with ShuffleNet, using
channel shuffling and group convolutions for greater efficiency,
but its performance drops noticeably on high resolution or
manipulation heavy datasets. Hinton et al. [14] proposed
knowledge distillation as a strategy to compress large models
into smaller student networks while preserving performance,
yet this approach typically requires GPU-intensive training
cycles, limiting practicality for low-resource environments.
Jacob et al. [15] further improved on-device inference by
introducing 8-bit quantization for integer-only execution, re-
ducing model size and latency with minimal accuracy degra-
dation. Nonetheless, precision decreases considerably when
processing subtle deepfake artifacts. Dolhansky et al. [16]
contributed the DFDC dataset, revealing the difficulty of real
world deepfake detection, as even top performing models
show limited generalization and require multi GPU training.
Altogether, hese works highlight the trade off between com-
putational efficiency and detection performance, emphasizing
the need for CPU optimized solutions that balance accuracy
and deployability.
In summary, it is evident from the explanation above that nu-
merous studies discussed their methods for deepfake detection
through a variety of approaches, including machine learning,
GPU-optimized techniques, and other advanced computational
models. These approaches represent the current state of the art
in deepfake detection with optimization techniques. Motivated
by these developments, we provide a real-time web-based
technique for detecting deepfakes through deep learning with
ResNet18 features extraction in a CPU-optimized manner to
ensure a practical and deployable solution.
III. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY FOR WEB-BASED
DEEPFAKE DETECTION
This system is organized into several layers, such as the
Input and Web Interface, Preprocessing Layer, Deepfake De-
tection with Deep Learning Layer, and the Database and Feed-
back Layer. Fig. 1 shows the overall workflow of the proposed
system. This presented methodology focuses on building an
end-to-end deepfake detection pipeline that can be practically
deployed on consumer-grade CPU hardware. Following sound
software engineering and security practices, the system is
well-structured as a complete web application that allows
users to upload images and videos, trigger analyses, and view
results and history in the browser. Importantly, the proposed
architecture starts with raw media input, standardizes 224 x
244 RGB images, and uses a ResNet18-based classifier trained
to distinguish real from fake faces. Around this core model,
the application logic layer enforces validation, access control,
and security controls, while a lightweight SQLite backend
stores analysis metadata and user activity. This modular design
allows the same detection engine to support single uploads,
batch analysis, and dashboard views, and it can be scaled
later to larger datasets or more powerful hardware without
changing the overall architecture. In addition to this, the data
flow Fig. 2 also highlights twelve stages, which involve image
upload, ResNet18 inference, and finally database storage, and
it takes a total of 1.21 seconds to process each image.
A. Data Preprocessing and Feature Selection
This system preprocessing pipeline is very concise. All the
images are resized to 224 × 224 pixels, which matches the
input requirements of ResNet-18. After resizing, each image
is normalized using ImageNet statistics.

B. Web Design Principles and Application
The web interface is built with Flask and Bootstrap 5 to
provide a simple and responsive design. As shown in Fig.
3, the system includes four main components: (a) the login
page, (b) the upload interface, (c) the results display, and (d)
the dashboard statistics. The design follows three principles.
First, simplicity users can upload media and view results with
minimal steps. Second, transparency prediction confidence and
processing time are clearly shown. Third, security all uploaded
files are validated and can be stored with encryption. The
workflow is straightforward: log in, upload a file, view the
prediction, and check the history page. The dashboard provides
a record of past analyses, allowing users to review results over
time.

D. Deepfake Detection Algorithm for the Presented Model
The proposed algorithm introduces a lightweight and CPU-
efficient pipeline for deepfake detection using a ResNet18
backbone. As presented in Algorithm 1, the procedure begins
with preprocessing, where each input, whether an image or a
video, is resized and normalized to the standard 224 × 224
RGB format required by ResNet18. The normalized input
is subsequently forwarded through the model to generate a
prediction probability p, which is evaluated against a fixed
threshold of 0.5 to determine whether the content is classified
as REAL or FAKE. For video analysis, the algorithm extracts
ten uniformly sampled frames and computes individual pre-
diction scores for each frame, after which the scores are
averaged to obtain a final, more robust classification. This
sequential process of preprocessing, feature extraction, and
probability-based decision-making ensures that the system
maintains accuracy, computational efficiency, and real-time
feasibility when deployed on standard CPU hardware.

E. CPU Optimized-based Deepfake Detection and Analysis
We employ ResNet18, a lightweight convolutional neu-
ral network with 11.3 million parameters, specifically cho-
sen for CPU-efficient inference. Deeper models (ResNet50,
ResNet101) achieve higher accuracy but require GPU ac-
celeration for practical inference times. ResNet18 provides
a pragmatic balance: on Intel Core i5 hardware, inference
completes in approximately 0.90 seconds per image, enabling

real-time analysis for single images and reasonable latency
(≈12 seconds) for video processing [19].
The model performs binary classification: deepfake (1) or
authentic (0). The final layer outputs a probability p ∈[0, 1]
via sigmoid activation. We apply a fixed threshold of 0.5:
predictions above 0.5 are classified as deepfakes, predictions
below are classified as authentic. Model training used the
Adam optimizer with learning rate 0.0005, binary cross-
entropy loss, and batch size 4 (limited by CPU memory con-
straints). Early stopping was implemented based on validation
loss, halting training after 5 epochs without improvement. The
best model achieved validation accuracy of 72.37%, precision
64.2%, recall 70.1%, and F1 score 67.0%.
This accuracy is modest compared to GPU-trained models
on larger datasets (which often exceed 90%). However, our
focus is on deployment feasibility, not maximum accuracy. A
72% model that runs on any laptop is more valuable in practice
than a 95% model requiring expensive hardware. The modest
accuracy also serves as a reminder: deepfake detection is fun-
damentally difficult, and even our best efforts are fallible. For
video input, we extract 10 frames as described earlier, process
each independently, and average the resulting probabilities.
This frame-level aggregation improves robustness compared to
analyzing a single frame, though a more sophisticated temporal
model (e.g., 3D CNN) could capture temporal consistency
patterns. We opted against this complexity to maintain CPU
compatibility. The model is deterministic: identical inputs al-
ways produce identical outputs. This predictability is valuable
for reproducibility and debugging, but it also means the model
cannot capture inherent uncertainty in borderline cases. Future
work might integrate uncertainty quantification (e.g., Monte
Carlo dropout) to express confidence in predictions [20].
IV. IMPLEMENTATION AND PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS
A. Environment Setup
The development environment is the same as for the general
consumer-grade hardware configuration to ensure accuracy
in the simulation. This includes the utilization of the Intel
Core i5-1135G7 processor (11th generation, 4 cores/8 threads
running at 2.4-4.2 GHz speeds) in a CPU-only setup for
the deep learning framework of PyTorch 2.0.1 running on
top of the Parrot Security OS. The Python version is 3.11.2,
supporting the framework of Flask 3.0.0.
B. Model Implementation and Training Configuration
Our model is ResNet18 pretrained on ImageNet, with a cus-
tom classification head replacing the original fully-connected
layer. The new head contains: a dense layer reducing 512
input features to 256 units, ReLU activation, dropout (0.5)
for regularization, then a final linear layer outputting a single
neuron with sigmoid activation. This produces a probability
score p ∈[0, 1], where p > 0.5 indicates fake classification.
Training used Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.0001, which
was reduced by factor 0.5 when validation loss plateaued for
2 epochs). Binary cross-entropy loss was minimized over 40
epochs with batch size 8. CPU memory constraints prevented
larger batches. Early stopping halted training after 5 epochs
without improvement. We applied mild augmentation: ran-
dom horizontal flipping (50% probability) and color jittering
(brightness/contrast ±0.2) to reduce overfitting.

V. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose a real-time system for the de-
tection of deepfakes that was designed to be executed on
CPUs in consumer-level hardware setups. The system achieves
a detection accuracy of 72.37% in real-time execution times
of less than two seconds, thus ensuring the effectiveness of
the detection even without the use of GPUs. Although the
accuracy of the approach falls below the benchmark of the
use of GPUs in the detection of deepfakes, the feasibility of
the approach in a real-world setup would allow a detection
accuracy of around 90% in a larger dataset. The web interface
demonstrates the capability to develop a complete detection
framework without relying on the utilization of specialized
hardware. In the future, we will focus on improving accuracy,
strengthening robustness, and supporting larger-scale deploy-
ment as deepfake technology continues to evolve.
