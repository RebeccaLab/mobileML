DeepFakes are widespread on social networks, and they result in severe information con-
cerns. Although various detection methods have been proposed, there are still practical
limitations. Previous speciﬁc artifact-based methods were insufﬁcient to capture ﬁne-
grained features, which limited their effectiveness against advanced DeepFakes. Current
DNN-based detectors tend to trade high costs for performance improvement, and are not
efﬁcient enough, given that DeepFakes can be created easily by mobile apps, and DNN-
based models require expensive computational resources. Furthermore, most methods lack
generalizability under the cross-dataset scenario. In this work, we instead mine the more
subtle and generalized defects of DeepFakes and propose the fused facial region_feature
descriptor (FFR_FD), which is only a vector of the discriminative feature description, for
effective and fast DeepFake detection. We show that DeepFake faces have fewer feature
points than real ones, especially in facial regions. FFR_FD capitalizes on such key observa-
tions, and thus has strong generalizability. We train a random forest classiﬁer with FFR_FD
to achieve efﬁcient detection. Extensive experiments on six large-scale DeepFake datasets
demonstrate the effectiveness of our lightweight method. Our model generalizes well on
the challenging Celeb-DF (v2) dataset, with 0.706 AUC, which is superior to most state-
of-the-art methods.

1. Introduction
Misinformation has become ubiquitous [1–3], a consequence of the ability to easily create multimedia content and share
it on social platforms. The remarkable progress of deep generative models has further led to the synthesis of super-realistic
images or videos. For example, the well-known DeepFake refers to swapping faces in a video through generative adversarial
networks (GANs) [4] or autoencoders (AEs) [5,6]. Some mobile apps (e.g., Avatarify1 and Reface2) also enable facial manipu-
lation. DeepFakes may be abused maliciously [7], such as by creating revenge pornography or striking at politicians. These real-
istic forgeries can easily deceive the human senses, raising concerns of privacy, social risks, and even national security.
Therefore, it is of paramount signiﬁcance to develop more efﬁcient and generalized methods for DeepFake detection.

Early DeepFakes generated by imperfect synthesis algorithms introduced noticeable visual artifacts, inspiring many
artifact-based detection methods [9–11] that adapted conventional machine learning models for speciﬁc forgery patterns
and were computationally efﬁcient. However, with improved means of generating DeepFakes, those artifact patterns no
longer hold. Hence, it is necessary to capture more ﬁne-grained discrepancies between real and fake faces, and this is a spe-
cialty of deep neural networks (DNNs), whose impressive classiﬁcation performance has spawned many DNN-based detec-
tors [12–16]. Nevertheless, these models directly take the original image pixels as input, and detection that entirely relies on
automatic feature extraction by DNNs is still vulnerable to more advanced DeepFakes [17]. Moreover, most of the above
methods are trained for a speciﬁc dataset, and cannot extrapolate to other datasets, i.e., they fail to generalize. To improve
the effectiveness and generalization of DeepFake detection, much recent work has introduced more powerful modules or
domain features to meticulously designed DNNs, such as the attention mechanism [18,19], texture features [20,21], and fre-
quency spectrum [22]. Concomitantly, to drive large and complex DNNs with large-scale datasets requires signiﬁcant com-
puting resources (e.g., GPUs) and training (e.g., parameter adjustment), which decreases efﬁciency.
Based on the above observations, DeepFake detection methods struggle to consider effectiveness, efﬁciency, and gener-
alization. To tackle these issues, we make the following arguments: 1) To improve detection effectiveness, more ﬁne-grained
forgery artifacts, which even exist in advanced DeepFakes, should be mined; 2) To improve generalization to unseen data-
sets, extracted features should be derived from the common defects of DeepFake generative models, and not be speciﬁc to
any dataset; 3) Considering the increasing incidence of DeepFakes and various forgery technologies, to train an efﬁcient
detection model is necessary. Bearing these observations in mind, we proposed to mine more generalizable clues for effec-
tive and fast DeepFake detection. From one aspect, it is feasible to capture the subtle artifacts implied in the local features of
DeepFakes. Image local feature detection-description algorithms have been successfully used in various vision applications
[23,24]. Algorithms to determine feature points by specifying the properties of images (e.g., edges and corners) are called
detectors, and those that describe detected points by neighboring pixels are referred to as descriptors. Inspired by the advan-
tages of feature point detector-descriptors, such as fast detection and low-dimensional yet discriminative descriptions, we
explore information about the number, distribution, and description of feature points in DeepFake faces.
In essence, deep generative models used for face swaps, such as autoencoders [6], require the reconstruction of faces from
latent features. However, the limited encoding space will cause the replaced face to be slightly blurred [13], which will affect
the feature point detector that makes decisions through pixel-level differences. Our experiments (Section 4.1) on self-
swapped faces and the widely used DeepFake datasets [10,25,16,26,27,17] reveal that the number of feature points in Deep-
Fake faces is generally less than in real ones, especially in the eyes, nose, mouth, and eyebrows. Indeed, due to the concen-
tration of details in these facial regions, the feature points of faces tend to be distributed in them, as shown on the left of
Fig. 1(a). Descriptors that record ﬁne-grained features such as gradient information [28] or intensity differences [29,30] will
also be affected by blur. However, each feature point of a face has one descriptor, making it arduous to detect DeepFakes
through a large number of messy descriptors [31,32]. Based on the above observations, we propose the fused facial
region_feature descriptor (FFR_FD), a compact vector representation for DeepFake detection.
FFR_FD can be constructed by various feature point detector-descriptors, and our experiments include SIFT [28], SURF
[33], FAST&BRIEF [8,29], ORB [30], and A-KAZE [34]. We divide feature points into eight facial regions: entire face, mouth,
inner_mouth, right_eyebrow, left_eyebrow, right_eye, left_eye, and nose. All of the feature point descriptors in each facial
region are used to construct a vector FDr for a region r, which can be controlled to introduce the quantity of information
of feature points by not taking the average value during the calculation. We connect FDr for each facial region in series to
form FFR_FD, which is consistent with the computational simplicity of feature detector-descriptors, and signiﬁcantly reduces
the dimension of features extracted from each face, from an N  d matrix to an 8  d vector, where N is the total number of
feature points in an image, and d is the descriptor’s dimension. To subdivide facial regions with FDr capitalizes on the lack of
feature points of DeepFakes in these regions, and is more discriminative than to directly use all descriptors in the entire face,
as shown on the right of Fig. 1(b).
We used FFR_FD as input to train a random forest classiﬁer. Extensive experiments were conducted on six DeepFake
benchmark datasets. Results demonstrate the effectiveness of our lightweight model for DeepFake detection. Speciﬁcally,
the frame-level AUC scores of the challenging DFD [26], DFDC [27], and Celeb-DF_V2 [17] datasets reached 85.2, 88.3,
and 82.2, respectively, outperforming many DNN-based detection methods. Moreover, our method has state-of-the-art
(SOTA) generalizability, which beneﬁts from the lack of feature points in current DeepFake faces. Frequently used notation
in this paper is summarized in Table 1. Our main contributions are as follows:
 Our empirical studies reveal that the use of deep generative models to swap faces will reduce the number of detected
feature points;
 The detection and statistical results of many large-scale datasets show that current DeepFake faces have fewer feature
points than real faces, especially in detailed regions. This substantial discrepancy is evident even on higher-quality datasets;
 We propose FFR_FD as an informative vector to represent the facial feature description, constructed by feature point
descriptors from subdivided facial regions. FFR_FD has computational simplicity and can be combined with various feature
point detector-descriptors;
 Random forest trained with FFR_FD can realize SOTA detection. Compared with popular DNN-based detection models,
our method is effective, more efﬁcient, and strongly generalizable.

2. Related work
We introduce DeepFake generation and detection methods that are related to our work.
2.1. DeepFake generation and datasets
The rapid progress of deep generative models presents signiﬁcant challenges to the quick and effective detection of real-
istic DeepFakes. Many DeepFake video benchmark datasets have been released to promote the research of DeepFake detec-
tion. Current DeepFake datasets basically comprise two generations [17]. The ﬁrst includes UADFV [10], DeepfakeTIMIT [25],
and FF++_DeepFakes [16], using FakeApp3, Faceswap-Gan4, and Faceswap [6] to generate fake videos. The second generation,
including DFD [26], DFDC [27], and Celeb_DF(V2) [17], improves visual quality through upgraded synthesis techniques. The data
scale of the second generation has been greatly increased, along with diversity, such as age, skin color, and lighting environ-
ment. Due to these advancements, consideration must be given to the cost and speed of a detection model in addition to its
performance.
2.2. DeepFake detection
Early work identiﬁed visual artifact patterns, such as inconsistent head poses [9], unnatural eye blinking [10], and corre-
lations between facial expressions and head movements [40]. These methods performed well for the time and considered the
consumption of the detector by training a simple classiﬁer such as two-class SVM [9,40]. However, those visible artifacts are
limited to speciﬁc forgeries. Therefore, detection models that adopt deep neural networks (DNNs) have gradually become
mainstream. For example, a two-stream CNN was used [12], Meso-4 focused on the mesoscopic properties of images [13],
a capsule structure based on VGG19 was used [14], ResNet was used to capture face warping artifacts [15], and classic Xcep-
tion was used to detect fake faces [16]. With powerful feature extraction capabilities, these DNN-based methods achieved
remarkable success, but they still struggle against advanced DeepFakes [17].
Great efforts have been made to apply learning-based detectors to this issue, and most have introduced more domain fea-
tures or powerful modules. Gram-Net [20] and InTeLe [21] explore the texture information of images to improve robustness.
F3-Net [38] uses two frequency-aware branches to extract frequency domain artifacts, with state-of-the-art results in highly
compressed settings. The attention mechanism can improve DNN ﬁtting ability, with more focus on critical features, show-
ing great progress in ﬁelds such as citywide trafﬁc ﬂow prediction [41] and DeepFake detection. Dang et al. [18] utilized an
attention map to locate forged regions and improve detection ability. MADD [19] highlights local parts guided by multi-

2.3. Analysis of existing methods
Table 2 compares current methods and the proposed method. Existing DeepFake detection methods can be categorized as
follows: 1) Methods without DNN use hand-crafted features for simple binary classiﬁers. These include some conventional
machine learning models [9,11,31,32] and shallow neural networks [13]. Most can operate efﬁciently on a consumer CPU.
Earlier work focused on dataset-speciﬁc artifacts, and were effective on DeepFakes of the time. However, they suffered
against second-generation DeepFakes, which had signiﬁcantly fewer obvious artifacts. Furthermore, speciﬁc artifacts are dif-
ﬁcult to extrapolate to unseen datasets, resulting in limited generalizability; 2) DNN-based methods are being widely
explored to improve the effectiveness of detection. Beneﬁting from the automatic feature extraction capability of DNN, these
methods can undoubtedly accomplish second-generation DeepFake detection, since they only need to be retrained and eval-
uated on the new dataset. Nevertheless, the powerful approximation capacity also limits the performance of most DNN-
based methods in cross-dataset evaluation. Recent efforts have noticed overﬁtting and introduced more general artifacts
such as common discrepancies around face-swapping boundaries [37]. In addition to detection performance, it is nontrivial
for DNN training to collect large-scale data against emerging forgeries. Models using data-driven DNNs are computationally
expensive and cannot be quickly trained using only a consumer CPU.
To meet these practical challenges, we mine the more ﬁne-grained and generalized feature defects of DeepFakes for effec-
tive and efﬁcient detection. We reveal that current DeepFake faces have fewer feature points than real ones, and empirically
show that this phenomenon stems from deep generative models for face swapping. Our proposed FFR_FD capitalizes on such
key observations, which apply to most current DeepFake datasets, and therefore are strongly generalizable. Driven by the
distribution of feature points and the discrepancies of descriptors, FFR_FD captures discriminative features in subdivided
facial regions for effective DeepFake detection. Moreover, FFR_FD is computationally efﬁcient due to the signiﬁcantly
reduced feature space. Since FFR_FD is a compact vector representation of the face, we use it to quickly train a random forest
classiﬁer and achieve efﬁcient detection.

3.1. SIFT
The scale invariant feature transform (SIFT) is a well-known feature detection and description algorithm [28]. It has four
steps: (1) scale-space extrema detection, i.e., the use of difference of Gaussians (DoG) to search for maxima at various scales
of the image; (2) keypoint localization, i.e., the location of potential feature points, where the Hessian matrix is used to
remove low-contrast points; (3) orientation assignment, i.e., the construction of an orientation histogram based on the gra-
dient information around the feature point, so as to assign an orientation to a feature point; and (4) the keypoint descriptor,
i.e., use of the gradient magnitude and orientation to construct a feature vector for feature points. This method extracts a
16  16 neighbor region in each feature point, and subdivides it into 4  4 sub-blocks with eight orientation bins, resulting
in a descriptor of 4  4  8 = 128 dimensions.
3.2. SURF
The feature detection of speeded up robust features (SURF) [33] is faster than that of SIFT. Due to the use of integral
images, the boxed ﬁlter approximates DOG. SURF uses the determinant of the Hessian matrix to ﬁnd the interest points
at blob-type structures. 2D Haar wavelet responses are used for orientation assignment and feature description. The neigh-
borhood around each feature point is divided into 4  4 subregions, whose wavelet responses have four values, so the
descriptor of each feature point is a vector of dimension 4  4  4 = 64.
3.3. FAST & BRIEF
Features from accelerated segment test (FAST) [8] is a high-speed feature detector developed for real-time applications.
Corner point extraction is performed by comparing the intensity thresholds between the point and pixels on a circular ring
around it. A decision tree based on the ID3 algorithm is trained as the corner detector. Non-maximum suppression is used to
solve the problem of adjacent corner points, which makes the FAST detector more efﬁcient. FAST is only a feature point
detector and does not involve feature description. Binary robust independent elementary features (BRIEF) [29] is a feature
point descriptor that uses simple binary strings. It performs Gaussian smoothing for the patch partition of detected feature
points, and randomly selects pairs of points for intensity difference tests to obtain descriptors. If the number of pairs of
points is 256, then the dimension of the descriptor is 32 bytes for each feature point, which is much smaller than that of
SIFT or SURF. BRIEF performs similarly to SIFT in most scenarios, but its low complexity makes the construction of descrip-
tors faster. Combining the FAST detector and BRIEF descriptor has the advantages of fast speed and low computational
resource requirements.
3.4. ORB
Introduced by Rublee et al. [30], oriented FAST and rotated BRIEF (ORB) solves the problem that the FAST detector does
not compute orientation and the BRIEF descriptor lacks rotational invariance. ORB applies the Harris corner measure to pick
the Top N points from FAST corners to evaluate cornerness and determine the local orientation by the intensity centroid [30].
ORB extracts the BRIEF descriptor according to the main orientation of the feature point. It adopts a greedy algorithm to ﬁnd
random pairwise pixel patches with low correlation. In this study, the authors selected 256 pairwise pixel patches for feature
description, so it generates a 32-dimensional descriptor. Note that we use the feature point information for DeepFake detec-
tion. The extracted faces have been cropped and aligned, so the improved properties of ORB have little impact on detection,
but we still use it for comparison.
3.5. A-KAZE
Introduced by Alcantarilla et al. [34], the A-KAZE feature detector and descriptor effectively constructs the nonlinear scale
space for feature detection through FED [43]. The descriptor is based on M-LDB, which is also efﬁcient at exploring gradient
information from the nonlinear scale space.

4.1. Swapping faces to reduce the number of feature points
Will deep generative models cause a change in the number of facial feature points? To this end, we utilize the original
faceswap model [6] for face swapping. The model is based on an autoencoder-decoder: the shared encoder is trained for
two faces, A and B, to extract the latent feature vectors, from which the corresponding faces are reconstructed by their
respective decoders. The compressed latent vectors can be understood as general face information such as the expression
and background, and the decoders are used to restore speciﬁc facial features, such as nose details, from latent vectors. After
training, the latent vectors extracted from A by the shared encoder are transmitted to the decoder of B, such that A is replaced
by B, as shown in Fig. 2.
Because the feature points of diverse facial appearances are naturally different, we use one face as both A and B, i.e., we
swap a face with itself. A real-face video containing 396 frames from FF++_YouTube [16] was used for experiments. After
sufﬁcient training of the model, we swapped the face with itself and detected the SIFT, SURF, FAST, ORB, and A-KAZE feature
points. The feature points of an original face and the corresponding fake face are shown in Fig. 3. Obviously, the fake face has
fewer feature points than the real face with the same feature point detector. This phenomenon is more noticeable in facial
regions with detailed information, such as the nose, mouth, eyes, and eyebrows. It can also be seen from Fig. 3 that while the
swapped face is similar to the original, many reﬁned details for determining feature points are lost.
To verify the observation results, we counted the numbers of feature points on the original and swapped faces from the
corresponding 369 video frames. The feature points in the facial images extracted from frames were summed, and the total
was divided by the number of faces to obtain the average number of feature points for each face. Based on the discrepancies
of the feature point distribution, we used Dlib [44] to subdivide the face into seven regions: mouth, inner_mouth, right_eye-
brow, left_eyebrow, right_eye, left_eye, and nose. We calculated the average number of feature points, as shown in Table 3.
These results again show that the Faceswap model will reduce the numbers of feature points of faces, especially in the sub-
divided facial regions. Compared to other detectors, the number of feature points of fake faces is reduced more signiﬁcantly
on FAST and ORB (based on modiﬁed FAST). Both visualizations and statistical results inspire us to explore the feature points
on detailed facial regions to detect forged faces.

5. Methodology
It is infeasible to directly judge authenticity by the number of feature points because this ﬂuctuates according to factors
such as facial appearance and video quality (compare FF++_DF (RAW) and FF++_DF (LQ) in Table 4). Hence, it is necessary to
explore the combination of feature point descriptors for detection, which is nontrivial if there are many feature points. If N
feature points are detected in a facial image, and the descriptor of each feature point is d-dimensional (depending on the
descriptor, as shown in Table 5), then one-shot will produce (N  d)-dimensional features. In current large-scale DeepFake
datasets, to directly train the classiﬁer with descriptors [31,32] or BOWs [45] (constructed from descriptors by k-means)
consumes much time and is prone to overﬁtting. We propose FFR_FD for fast, effective DeepFake detection.

The time complexity of Algorithm 1 strongly depends on the face detector, feature point detector-descriptor, and number
of feature points. If l is the time of face detection, m is the time of detecting all feature points in a facial image, and n is the
time of generating descriptors for each feature point, then the complexity is Oðl þ m þ ðn þ 1ÞNÞ. The algorithm obtains facial

are traversed and divided into facial regions to construct FDr, which is OðNÞ, and the concatenation of the FDr to obtain
FFR_FD is constant.
FFR_FD has the following characteristics: 1) It integrates the feature descriptions of all subdivided facial regions; 2) Its
calculations are efﬁcient. FFR_FD can be constructed by combining any feature point detector and descriptor, so its distin-
guishability and speed beneﬁt from the feature point algorithm; 3) It can control whether it is affected by the number of
feature points; 4) Only a low-dimensional vector (8  d) is used to describe the facial feature information, which signiﬁcantly
reduces the dimension compared to the original facial image (256  256) or feature descriptors (N  d), as shown in Table 5.
5.2. Differences in FFR_FD between real and fake faces
If the number of real images in a training set is NR, then NR FFR_FDs are generated. We calculate their means and variances
on the 8  d dimensions, and similarly, NF for fake images. In a given dimension, we subtract the statistical results of fake
images from those of real ones to obtain the statistical differences in each dimension, as shown in Fig. 5 (without averaging
FDr). In UADFV, the mean values of FFR_FDs constructed by the SIFT detector-descriptor for real faces are larger than those of
fake faces in all dimensions, especially in the entire face and mouth regions, as shown in Fig. 5(a). Note that SIFT descriptors
are based on gradients; hence, the gradients around the feature points of real faces are stronger than those of fake faces.
Fig. 5(b) shows that the real FFR_FDs have a greater variance in the entire face, i.e., their ﬂuctuation is more signiﬁcant. Fea-
ture points detected by FAST are described by BRIEF. In Fig. 5(c), in the mouth, inner_mouth, right_eye, and left_eye regions,
the mean values of the real faces are larger, i.e., the intensity differences used to construct the BRIEF descriptor are greater,
and the opposite is true in the right_eyebrow, left_eyebrow, and nose regions. Fig. 5(d) illustrates that the real faces have
greater intensity difference ﬂuctuations, especially in the mouth and inner_mouth. Similar analyses can be performed on
other datasets, such as Celeb-DF_V2, as shown at the bottom of Fig. 5. We conclude that real facial images and DeepFakes
are fairly different in FFR_FD, and the speciﬁc distribution is affected by the feature point detector-descriptor.

We tried other models, such as LR, SVM, ELM, and MLP, and found that random forest performs best in our experiments,
as it does not easily overﬁt. Random forest works well with high-dimensional features, and has a strong adaptability to fea-
tures and a fast training speed, which is compatible with the characteristics of FFR_FD. Also, random forest can measure the
importance of features, which has some reference signiﬁcance. Hence, the random forest classiﬁer presents many advantages
for our proposed FFR_FD in DeepFake detection:
 It can handle high-dimensional data without handcrafted feature selection, which accords with the feature description
of FFR_FD;
 It is relatively robust to noise, which is helpful given that some FFR_FDs are constructed from poor-quality DeepFake
faces;
 It provides feature importance in classiﬁcation, which explains crucial facial regions for detection;
 It is computationally efﬁcient on large datasets due to the parallelization of subtrees in training, which is consistent
with the motivation of FFR_FD for fast detection.

6.1. Experimental setup
Datasets
We evaluated six widely-used ﬁrst- and second-generation DeepFake datasets in our experiments. The ﬁrst-generation
included DeepfakeTIMIT (HQ and LQ) [25], UADFV [10], and FF++_DeepFake (RAW and LQ) [16], and the second-
generation datasets were DFD [26], DFDC [27], and Celeb-DF_V2 [17]. Each dataset was split into 80% for training and the
rest for testing. For Celeb-DF_V2, we followed the ofﬁcially speciﬁed label [17] to divide the testing set. The facial datasets
for our experiment are shown in Table 6; to prevent data leakage, facial images were extracted after the original videos were
split into training and testing sets. DeepFakeTIMIT and UADFV provide fewer frames, so all were used. In all other datasets,
we only used a small part of the frames in each video. The data scale was reduced to take advantage of the efﬁciency of
FFR_FD and evaluate whether our method required as much data as deep CNN.
Implementation
FFR_FD We used the feature detector-descriptors of SIFT, SURF, FAST&BRIEF, ORB, and A-KAZE to construct FFR_FD for
comparison. To investigate the inﬂuence of quantity information of feature points for detection, we compared the detection
results of FFR_FD obtained both by averaging and not averaging FDr, which are denoted as ave and no_ave, respectively.
Hyperparameter settings We report the frame-level ROC-AUC score as the metric, which is widely used in DeepFake
detection. In our experiments, 20% of the training data were sequentially split as the validation subset for ﬁvefold cross-
validation. The two most crucial parameter deﬁnitions in the random forest classiﬁer are the number of decision subtrees
T and max feature F (i.e., the number of features for each node to grow a subtree). Breiman [46] pointed out that increasing
the number of subtrees will not lead to overtraining, since the generalization error always converges. As shown in Table 5,
the feature dimension of the proposed FFR_FD is not very large, so we set the range of T only to include 200, 500, 800, and
adjusted the number of decision trees according to the optimal metrics reported on the validation subsets. As for the max
feature F, although it can be increased to improve the strength of each tree in the model, its reduction will also beneﬁt from
the reduced correlation between trees in terms of the ensemble. We developed the random forest classiﬁer with scikit-learn
[47], which provides the auto mode in the RandomForest class to automatically select the optimal F. Under this setting, m was
ﬁxed at

6.2. Comparison with previous methods
We evaluate our method on six DeepFake datasets and compare FFR_FD with previous detection methods, as shown in
Table 7. Among ﬁve detector-descriptors, the overall detection performance of FFR_FD extracted by the FAST detector com-
bined with the BRIEF descriptor was most effective and stable. As described in Section 4, the number of feature points of fake
faces detected by FAST was signiﬁcantly less. However, ORB with the same signiﬁcant reduction in the number of feature
points did not perform ideally. ORB is based on FAST&BRIEF to improve scale- and rotation-invariance, but all faces were
cropped and aligned in the DeepFake detection task. In this case, scale- and rotation-invariance weaken the discriminability
of FFR_FD. As shown in Table 7, the introduction of feature point quantity information did not improve AUC in some cases,
indicating that the feature point descriptors themselves are sufﬁciently distinguishable. Comparing the detection results of
the RAW and c40 (LQ) versions of FF++_DF, our method was affected slightly by strong compression, but was still acceptable.
On the DT (LQ), UADFV, and extremely challenging DFDC and Celeb-DF_V2 datasets, our detection performance was ahead of
all methods, and it was competitive on other datasets.

further introduced a spatial pyramid pooling module to solve multiscale issues. This method of exposing face warping arti-

6.3. Generalization ability evaluation
Although DNN-based models showed excellent DeepFake detection performance, many are dataset-speciﬁc and cannot
extrapolate to unseen datasets. To evaluate the generalization ability of the proposed FFR_FD, we conducted cross-dataset
experiments, in which the classiﬁer was trained on one dataset and tested on others. In this setting, we only used the random
forest with 200 subtrees trained by DeepfakeTIMIT (HQ) as the classiﬁer, and all the other datasets were used as testing sets
to evaluate generalization. The task is nontrivial because DeepfakeTIMIT (HQ) is a ﬁrst-generation DeepFake dataset with
few frames available. Other datasets had more DeepFake videos, of better quality. We believe we are the ﬁrst to test gener-
alization in this challenging setting. ResNet50 [48], Xception [49], and EfﬁcientNet-B0 [50] are SOTA DNN-based DeepFake
detectors, so we chose them as baselines. We implemented baseline models with Keras and TensorFlow libraries, and only
modiﬁed the fully-connected layers for binary classiﬁcation.
The generalization results of baselines and FFR_FD (constructed by FAST&BRIEF) are shown in Table 8. ResNet50, Xcep-
tion, and EfﬁcientNet-B0 achieved perfect AUC scores on DT (LQ), slightly better than 98.5% of the result of our FFR_FD.
Indeed, these deep CNNs effectively extract discriminative features, but they are limited to in-domain data. Comparing

6.4. Comparison with other SOTA methods on Celeb-DF_V2
The Celeb-DF_V2 dataset improves the forgery quality and signiﬁcantly reduces visible artifacts compared to other data-
sets, and hence is more challenging to generalize. Following convention [17,19], we trained the model on FF++ and tested it
on Celeb-DF_V2 (i.e., no_ave FFR_FD constructed from FF++ was used to train the model, and no_ave FFR_FD from Celeb-
DF_V2 was used to test it), to evaluate the transferability of our method. The frame-level average AUC score is shown in
Fig. 7. Compared with the recently proposed MADD [19], F3-Net [38], and SMIL [39], FFR_FD further improved the metrics
by 3.12%, 6.27%, and 14.26%, respectively. The results demonstrate that our method achieves SOTA generalizability. Behind
this improvement on the challenging Celeb-DF_V2, the facial regions of forged faces lack sufﬁcient feature points, although
the synthesis algorithms are enhanced. Moreover, the feature importances of FFR_FD focused by random forest actually show
a similar distribution on FF++ and Celeb-DF_V2, as shown in Fig. 8(d) and (h). These provide new insights into the resulting
gains of generalization performance.

7. Efﬁciency of FFR_FD
In our experiments, both the construction of FFR_FD and the training of random forest were performed on a consumer-
level Intel Core i7-9700 K CPU with 32 GB RAM. Fig. 10(a) shows the average calculation time to construct an FFR_FD from a
256  256 facial image. The quantitative comparison illustrates that the computational efﬁciency of the feature detection-
descriptor for computing FFR_FD is ranked as FAST&BRIEF > ORB > A-KAZE > SURF > SIFT. As expected, it depends on the
calculation speed of the feature point algorithm [24].
We randomly used 10 K FFR_FDs (i.e., extracted from 10 K images) to train a random forest classiﬁer with 500 subtrees,
and compared average times of the training procedure. As shown in Fig. 10(b), the training time is positively related to the
dimension of FFR_FD. In our experimental environment, if 100 K facial images are used and FFR_FD is constructed from FAS-
T&BRIEF, it takes less than one hour to complete the construction and training process. Mainstream DNN-based models are
competitive in DeepFake detection tasks. However, even with the help of a GPU, driving 100 K facial images to train deep
neural networks consumes more time than our method.
8. Limitations and conclusion
We presented FFR_FD, a vector representation for DeepFake detection, which can be constructed from different facial
regions in combination with various feature descriptors. Inspired by local feature detection-description algorithms to extract
ﬁne-grained features, we explored the feature points in DeepFakes. Using feature point detector-descriptors SIFT, SURF, FAS-

Appendix A. Normalization of FFR_FD
In our experiments, FFR_FD does not need to be normalized, given that feature point descriptors have been normalized.
SIFT, BRIEF, ORB, and A-KAZE are all normalized at [0, 255], and the SURF descriptor based on the sum of the Haar wavelet
response is at [1, 1]. We construct FDr by accumulating the same type descriptors in the facial region r, and the resulting
FFR_FDs have dimensional uniﬁcation. Compared with logistic regression, support vector machine, and multilayer percep-
tron, random forest is the best classiﬁer according to our test results. It does not need to standardize the features, given that
the decision tree does not involve distance measurement.
